The business in five charts

Large revenues, small partnerships and several ways to divide the proceeds.

The RBB Economics group accounts for the year to March 2025 record a £5.77m profit entitlement for one unnamed member. Dividing the £60.2m profit pool by the average membership of 18 gives £3.35m per average member, before personal tax.

Public accounts reveal how differently consultancies organise the same scarce resource: expert labour. They are less good at telling us how large the economics trade is. A broad adviser’s accounts may include restructuring, technology and forensic work alongside economics.

A catalogue, not a census

The project follows 90 firms associated with UK economics consultancy. Policy and regulation account for most names; some of the largest revenue observations belong to broad advisers and litigation practices. The categories describe each firm’s place in this catalogue, not its share of a measured market.

Scale comes in bundles

Thirty catalogue entries have a positive revenue observation in the 24 July 2026 financial snapshot, checked against source accounts in September. They sum to £2.4415bn, with year-ends from July 2024 to December 2025. This includes overseas operations and non-economics work, and uses AlixPartners’ net revenue. It is not UK sector revenue or a market floor.

Four observations exceed £250m: FTI Consulting, Baringa, AlixPartners UK and A&M Europe. FTI’s £460.8m covers twelve months to March 2025; its £550.5m comparative covered fifteen months. AlixPartners’ £273.2m is after client expenses. A&M’s €301.3m becomes £256.1m at £0.85 per euro. None is a clean measure of its economics practice.

Cebr estimated UK economics consultancy revenue at £1.53bn in 2016/17, with nominal annual growth averaging 11.3% over the preceding three years. Its estimate covers UK sector activity. The chart above reports a selection of company and group accounts.

The line matters

RBB’s operating margin was 56.3%; Oxera’s was 38.4%. Both figures precede member profit allocations. Further down the income statement, Oxera charged £8.3m as member remuneration. Its remaining discretionary profit was 26.5% of revenue; RBB’s was 55.6%.

Moving between those lines changes the comparison. It does not reveal each owner’s total reward. Salaries, remuneration expenses, profit allocations and dividends are different things; geography and business mix differ too.

A smaller firm, a faster year

RBB’s revenue rose from £82.0m to £108.4m between FY2024 and FY2025. Baringa’s rose from £432.8m to £449.8m. That is growth of 32.2% and 3.9% respectively. The accounts establish the change, not whether hiring, prices or demand caused it.

How many members share the profit?

RBB leads the four observations below in discretionary profit per average member. Baringa’s accounts report a much larger profit pool and an average of 178 members. Its top member’s allocation is estimated at £11.3m from a disclosed 7% share, excluding a separate payment tied to business-disposal cash receipts.

The chart uses four source-checked, positive discretionary profit pools from accounts that also disclose average LLP membership. Oxera and Macro Advisory Partners report their residual pools after charging members’ remuneration.

Three careers

Career moves connect the firms, but a team announcement and a shared Companies House officer are different observations. These selected events have no numerical weights. They illustrate routes into consulting; they cannot measure a net drain from regulators or rank the best-connected firm.
