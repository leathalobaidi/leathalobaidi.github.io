The Founders’ Decade, 2000–2010

Part 2 of 7: a specialist partnership, employee ownership and a corporate acquisition programme.

Leath · Revised November 2026

RBB Economics began with sixteen people in 2002. Its three founders, Derek Ridyard, Simon Bishop and Simon Baker, had worked together at NERA. They left an established international firm to create a practice devoted to competition economics.

Frontier Economics had made a different choice three years earlier: share ownership with the people doing the work. Across the Atlantic, FTI Consulting was about to buy two economics firms and combine them. The decade's important distinction was not simply between old firms and new ones. It was between ways of owning an expert business.

Frontier’s choice

Frontier’s company was incorporated on 15 April 1999. Simon Gaysford and Daniel Elliott were among its founders. Its first accounts name directors including Philip Burns, Michael Webb and Zoltan Biro. The accounts for the following year, to April 2001, report nineteen average employees in the 2000 comparative column.

In his account of the founding, Gaysford describes giving equity to colleagues when the firm was created. He connects the decision to his own experience: advancement should depend on the work people produced, rather than their background. That is a founder's explanation of his choices, not an independent test of how the firm behaved.

Ownership gave the principle a practical form. Employees would share in a business they helped build. Frontier continues to describe itself as staff-owned. Salaries, bonuses and ownership returns may distribute value differently from an LLP’s residual profit. The financial comparison must account for those differences.

The firm also grew abroad, opening in Cologne in 2003. Employee ownership had not confined it to a single London office. The later career records cannot isolate ownership’s effect on continuity.

The NERA departure

RBB’s own history dates its launch to 2002 and names sixteen people at the start. Its three founders were Derek Ridyard, Simon Bishop and Simon Baker. An RBB representative’s later evidence to Australia’s Productivity Commission confirms that they had worked together in NERA’s competition team.

Ridyard had led NERA’s European competition practice. Bishop had co-written The Economics of E.C. Competition Law with Mike Walker. The new firm therefore began with an established body of expertise, rather than waiting for a new generation to acquire it.

CRA’s London-office announcement of 14 August 2000 named Christopher Doyle, Robert Laslett and Mike Walker, credited as Michael Walker, as leaders in telecommunications, financial services and competition, respectively. It reported about fifteen further employees in addition to those three vice-presidents. Walker later moved from CRA to the CMA and joined Frontier in January 2026.

RBB’s current LLP, OC315356, was incorporated on 26 September 2005. That registration is distinct from both the 2002 launch and the partnership transition which RBB dates to 2007. The legal and commercial histories need separate dates.

Later accounts show revenue rising from £59.2m in FY2020 to £60.7m in FY2021, with twenty average members in both years. They do not tell us what the founders expected in 2002, or how much of the subsequent growth arose from ownership rather than clients, recruitment or market conditions.

FTI buys the expertise

FTI followed an acquisition route. Its 2003 annual report records the purchase of Chicago’s Lexecon Inc for about $129.2m. This was the American business founded in 1977, not Bill Bishop’s legally separate London Lexecon Ltd.

Competition Policy Associates, known as COMPASS, was founded in 2003 by Margaret Guerin-Calvert, Janusz Ordover, Jonathan Orszag, Peter Orszag and Robert Willig. On 6 January 2006, FTI completed its acquisition. The initial recorded cost was about $73.9m: $48.2m in cash and stock valued at $25.7m. The amount was an initial cost, with further adjustments and contingent payments possible.

Lexecon and COMPASS combined in January 2008 as Compass Lexecon. FTI had bought a body of specialist knowledge and given it a common brand within a larger advisory group. The purchase prices describe those transactions, not the value of a UK economics practice.

Room for specialists

The new firms did not all seek merger work. The company now called Cambridge Economic Policy Associates was incorporated in September 2000. Its later infrastructure finance research illustrates an adjacent policy market. Vivid Economics, founded by Cameron Hepburn and Robin Smale in 2006, concentrated on climate and environmental economics. Their emergence widened the range of problems on which a specialist consultancy could sell advice.

It is harder to put a reliable size on the changes. Some early small-company accounts omit turnover; others cover different periods or legal entities. The sequence of openings, departures and ownership decisions is clearer than a reconstructed league table.

By 2010, Frontier had grown under employee ownership, RBB had established a focused partnership, and FTI had assembled Compass Lexecon. None was a universal template. A broad advisory parent could buy expertise; a founder-led firm could try to keep its owners close to the work.

LECG provided the next test. Its listed parent had expanded and merged with SMART Business Advisory. Its 2009 annual report recorded 806 employees before the SMART combination. Within the next two years it would sell practices and wind down. The durability of an economics business depended on more than the standing of its experts.
