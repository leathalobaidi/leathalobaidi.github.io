A tour of telecommunications regulation — from the copper local loop to all-IP fibre. Why the "last mile" is the bottleneck, how unbundling and interconnection open the network, and the live fights over mobile termination, fibre investment, regulatory holidays and net neutrality.

Welcome to the longest chapter in the book, and arguably the most consequential — telecommunications is where the modern restructuring era began, with the 1984 AT&T break-up in the US and BT's privatisation in the UK. Decker calls the last thirty years a "revolution", and he means it: thirty years ago the only way to reach a remote person instantly was a fixed-line call, and today you have mobile, SMS, VoIP, video and a dozen internet channels. Key terms

two-way connection · network externality · convergence · triple/quad play

10.1.1 · CHARACTERISTICS OF THE PRODUCT

Not electrons in a pipe — a two-way handshake.

Electricity, gas and water are one-way flows of anonymous, homogeneous units. Telecoms is different: the product is a two-way interconnection between named parties, often across networks of different types.

Two-way, non-anonymous

A call needs a caller and a receiver, sometimes on a fixed net and a mobile net with different quality. Carriage between two identifiable users — not a commodity you can trade wholesale.

Network externalities

Each new user makes the network more valuable to everyone already on it. In 1994 there were 2m computers online; by 2012, 2.4bn internet users. This "bandwagon" logic drives some access-price choices.

Convergence. Digitisation and IP have split the network from the service. One infrastructure now carries voice, data and TV, so operators sell "triple" or "quadruple play" bundles — and different infrastructures now compete head-to-head.
Start with what makes telecoms genuinely different from the other utilities in this book. Electricity, gas and water are one-way transport of anonymous units — you don't care which electron reaches your kettle. Telecoms carries a two-way connection between specific, named people, and often across two different networks with different characteristics, which means the "product" can be genuinely heterogeneous. That's why, as we'll see, no wholesale commodity market ever emerged in telecoms the way it did in electricity. Second big idea: network externalities. The value of the network to you rises with the number of people you can reach — the classic telephone example — and regulators have sometimes exploited this by loading access charges to fund subscriber growth. Third, and this is the through-line of the whole chapter: convergence. Digitisation and internet protocol have prised apart the underlying network from the services riding on it, so one pipe now carries voice, data and video, and cable, fixed and mobile networks increasingly compete. Hold "convergence" in your head; it reappears on almost every slide.

Key terms

star topology · local exchange · trunk network · tandem switch · circuit switching

10.1.2 · PSTN ARCHITECTURE

The old phone network is a star.

The Public Switched Telephone Network was built for one-to-one voice. Each user is wired to a local exchange; exchanges connect over a trunk network via tandem switches. A star beats a full mesh — far fewer wires.

Circuit switching. A dedicated calling path is set up at dial-up and held for the whole call. Congestion only bites at set-up — no dial tone — never mid-call.
This is Decker's Figure 10.1 and it repays a slow read. The PSTN was designed for one thing — carrying one-to-one human voice — and its whole shape reflects that. Every subscriber runs a wire to a local exchange, the exchange acts as a switchboard connecting your wire to the wire of whoever you're calling, and a second tier of "tandem" switching links exchanges over long distances through the trunk network. Why a star and not a full mesh? Because permanently wiring every phone to every other phone would need an astronomical, impractical number of connections; the star radically cuts the transmission paths and therefore the cost. The other thing to notice is circuit switching: when you dial, the network reserves a dedicated electronic path and holds it for the whole call, so the only place congestion can bite is at set-up — you either get a dial tone or you don't, but once connected the quality is guaranteed. Above all, mark those red links on the left and right — the local loop, the last-mile wire from the exchange to your premises. That is the piece we'll return to again and again: it is the bottleneck of the whole industry.

NGN · packet switching · IP · latency & jitter · managed vs best-efforts

10.1.2 · NGN ARCHITECTURE

The new network is all packets, all IP.

Next-Generation Networks flip the design: the network is built for data, and voice becomes just another data stream. Everything is digital, packet-switched, and routed by the Internet Protocol.

Managed vs "best-efforts". Managed packet networks prioritise time-sensitive voice packets to hold call quality; "over-the-top" VoIP (Skype, Vonage) rides the public internet on a best-efforts basis — great when uncongested, unreliable when not.
Now contrast the PSTN with what's replacing it — the Next-Generation Network. There's no single agreed definition, but three features nail it down: NGNs are all-digital, they use packet switching rather than circuit switching, and they route those packets using the Internet Protocol. The intuition for packet switching: instead of reserving a private lane for your call, the network chops everything — including voice — into little addressed envelopes, sends them across shared trunks by whatever route is free, and reassembles them at the far end. That's far more efficient because you're not holding bandwidth idle during the silences in a conversation. The cost is a new kind of risk: at times of congestion packets can be delayed, arrive out of order, or be discarded — latency and jitter — which is fine for email but ruinous for a live voice or video call. The industry's answer is "managed" packet networks that prioritise time-sensitive traffic to preserve quality, versus over-the-top VoIP like Skype, which rides the public internet on a best-efforts basis where every packet is treated alike. Gillan and Malfara put the reversal beautifully: the old network was designed for voice with data bolted on; with IP the roles flip — the network is designed for data, and voice is just an application.

Key terms

twisted copper · DSL · fibre (FTTx) · HFC coax · radio spectrum

10.1.3 · TRANSMISSION MEDIUMS & THE LOCAL LOOP

Four ways to reach the home — the last mile is the prize.

Copper

Twisted pairs, built for voice. Limited bandwidth, but DSL upgrades it — quality falls with distance to the exchange (~2 miles max).

Fibre

Glass, light signals, huge bandwidth. Now replacing copper deeper into the access net — FTTN/C/B/H.

HFC coax

Cable operators: fibre to a node, then coaxial to the home. DOCSIS added data & voice.

Radio

Mobile: spectrum links handset → base station → switching centre. 3G ~5 Mbps, 4G/LTE 20–30 Mbps.

The bottleneck lives here. The local loop — the last mile from exchange to premises — is expensive to duplicate and hard to bypass. Hatfield et al. note all three fixed mediums converge on the same shape: fibre to a node near you, then copper/coax/radio the final stretch.
To understand where regulation bites, you have to understand the physical mediums that reach the customer, because the economics of the last mile drive everything. Four options. Copper twisted pairs — the legacy of the entire PSTN — were built for narrowband voice, but DSL technology squeezes high-speed data out of them, with the crucial caveat that quality degrades sharply with distance, so DSL only works within a couple of miles of the exchange. Fibre-optic cable carries light, has vastly more bandwidth, and is steadily being pushed closer to the home — the alphabet soup of FTTN, FTTC, FTTB, FTTH just measures how close. Cable companies use hybrid fibre-coaxial: fibre to a neighbourhood node, coax for the final run. And mobile uses radio spectrum from your handset to the nearest base station. Here's the punchline in the callout, and it's the single most important economic fact in the chapter: the local loop, the last mile, is the enduring bottleneck. It's ruinously expensive to lay a second set of wires to every home, so whoever controls the loop controls access to the customer. Notice too the convergence point — all three fixed technologies are evolving toward the same shape: high-capacity fibre to a node near you, then a cheaper medium for the last stretch.

Key terms

voice · data · broadcasting · dial-up vs broadband · cord-cutting

10.1.3 · SERVICES AND CONVERGENCE

One pipe, three service families.

Voice

Fixed PSTN, IP/managed VoIP, over-the-top VoIP, and mobile. In the US ~20% have "cut the cord" — mobile-only, no fixed line.

Data

Dial-up narrowband (≤56 Kbps) largely displaced by broadband — DSL, cable modem, fibre, and wireless (Wi-Fi, WiMax, 3G/4G).

Broadcasting

Cable TV historically separate; now IPTV and video-on-demand ride the same broadband connection (iPlayer, Hulu, Netflix).

Convergence in one sentence. Services are no longer tied to a platform — telephone, cable and wireless firms now all offer voice, data and TV. That's what creates the potential for facilities-based (infrastructure-vs-infrastructure) competition.

Decker's transport analogy: just as freight and passenger trains share one rail network, so voice, data and video "packets" now share one telecoms infrastructure — and, like rail vs road, different infrastructures compete.

With the mediums in place, look at the services riding on them, which fall into three families. Voice can now travel four ways — traditional fixed PSTN, managed IP telephony, over-the-top VoIP like Skype, and mobile — and the demand shift is dramatic: in the US around a fifth of consumers have cut the cord entirely and gone mobile-only, and in many developing countries mobile leapfrogged fixed altogether. Data has moved from dial-up narrowband, capped around 56 kilobits, to always-on broadband over DSL, cable, fibre and wireless. And broadcasting, once a separate cable product, is dissolving into the broadband connection through IPTV and video-on-demand — iPlayer, Hulu, Netflix. The unifying idea is convergence: services are no longer welded to a particular platform, so a phone company, a cable company and a mobile operator all now sell overlapping bundles. That's what creates the possibility of facilities-based competition — rival infrastructures competing to serve the same customer — which is the central hope of modern telecoms policy. Decker's transport analogy is the one to remember: just as different trains share one rail network and rail competes with road, different data packets now share one telecoms network and different networks compete.

10.1.3 · SERVICE PROVIDERS & 10.1.4 TRADING

Incumbents, entrants, cable and mobile.

Fixed incumbents

The privatised former monopolies — BT (UK), Verizon & AT&T (US), Deutsche Telekom, France Télécom, Telstra — plus entrant operators who built infrastructure after entry was liberalised.

Cable companies

Started in TV, added switching in the 1990s. Comcast and Time Warner Cable are now among the world's largest fixed broadband providers.

Mobile operators (MNOs)

Usually an oligopoly of 3–4 nation-wide carriers, gated by spectrum licences.

MVNOs & OTT

Virtual operators resell wholesale capacity with no spectrum; over-the-top players (Skype) and IPTV (iPlayer, Hulu) add another layer.

No commodity market — plus spectrum. Unlike electricity, no wholesale trading market emerged: the "product" is a connection. Wholesale trade is instead about interconnection terms. The one capacity-type market is radio spectrum — allocated by auction or "beauty contest" (India's 3G auction raised ~$11bn).

Who actually supplies these services matters, because historically different providers faced very different regulation. Start with the fixed incumbents — the privatised former state monopolies: BT in Britain, Verizon and AT&T in the States, Deutsche Telekom, France Télécom, Telstra. Alongside them sit entrant operators who built their own infrastructure once statutory entry barriers fell. Then cable companies, which began life carrying television, bolted on telephone switching in the 1990s, and today Comcast and Time Warner Cable rank among the biggest fixed broadband providers on earth. Mobile is typically an oligopoly of three or four national carriers, because spectrum licences are scarce, and beneath them sit MVNOs — virtual operators who own no spectrum and simply resell wholesale capacity — plus the over-the-top and IPTV players. Two structural points close section 10.1. First, because the product is a connection between two named users, no wholesale commodity market ever emerged as it did in electricity — wholesale trade is about interconnection terms instead. Second, the one genuine capacity market is radio spectrum, allocated by auction or by "beauty contest"; India's 3G auction alone raised roughly eleven billion dollars, and secondary trading lets new entrants buy their way into the mobile market.

MFJ 1984 · duopoly policy · Telecom Act 1996 · forbearance

10.2.1 · REGULATION OF THE PSTN

Four stages: monopoly → duopoly → full competition → forbearance.

  • Stage 1 · pre-1984 — Classic natural monopoly. A single PSTN operator (state-owned or regulated) with end-to-end responsibility. Focus on retail prices, universal service and cross-subsidy.
  • Stage 2 · mid-1980s — Restructuring. US MFJ 1984 breaks up AT&T into seven RBOCs; UK & Australia run a duopoly policy to grow one rival. Interconnection now needs a framework.
  • Stage 3 · mid-1990s — Competition in all activities, including the local loop. US Telecom Act 1996 mandates unbundling; UK requires BT to unbundle its loop (1999). Access pricing becomes the battleground.
  • Stage 4 · 2000s+ — As competition matures, regulate only residual bottlenecks (the loop), withdraw the rest. UK lifts BT retail price controls (2006); EU halves its list of regulated markets.
Now we pivot from description to regulation, and the cleanest way in is Decker's four-stage history of PSTN regulation. Stage one, up to the mid-1980s: the industry is treated as a classic natural monopoly, a single operator either state-owned or regulated, and the regulator's job is mainly setting retail prices, guaranteeing universal service, and running political cross-subsidies — line rentals and local calls kept cheap, long-distance kept dear. Stage two arrives with restructuring: in 1984 the US Modified Final Judgment breaks AT&T into seven Regional Bell Operating Companies, while the UK and Australia instead run a "duopoly policy", licensing one rival to grow into an effective competitor. Suddenly you need a framework for how incumbent and entrant interconnect. Stage three, from the mid-1990s, is the big one: policy pushes competition into every activity, crucially the local loop, via unbundling — the US Telecom Act of 1996, the UK's 1999 requirement that BT unbundle. Here access pricing becomes the central and most contested question in the whole subject. Stage four, the 2000s onward, is a partial retreat: as competition matures, regulate only the residual bottleneck and withdraw elsewhere — Britain scrapped BT's retail price controls in 2006, and the EU halved its list of markets subject to ex-ante regulation.

Key terms

LLU · interconnection · essential facility · margin squeeze

10.2.1 · UNBUNDLING THE LOCAL LOOP

Make the incumbent rent out its last mile.

If you can't economically duplicate the loop, force the incumbent to share it. Under LLU, an entrant installs its own kit in the exchange and leases the copper wire to each home — competing on service over the incumbent's bottleneck.

Watch the squeeze. An incumbent can raise the wholesale loop price while cutting its own retail price, "squeezing" the entrant's margin. Regulating that gap is a core LLU task.
Here is the single most important regulatory mechanism in fixed-line telecoms — local-loop unbundling. The logic follows straight from the bottleneck: you can't sensibly build a second copper network to every home, so instead of forcing wasteful duplication you compel the incumbent to share the loop it already owns. Read the diagram. The entrant places its own electronics inside the incumbent's local exchange — beside the incumbent's kit — and leases the individual copper wire running out to each subscriber. The entrant then hauls the traffic back to its own network and competes on price, service and features, all over the incumbent's last mile. This is quasi-facilities competition: the entrant invests in some kit but rents the bottleneck. In the US it flowed from the 1996 Act, in the UK from Ofcom's 1999 decision requiring BT, and in Europe from the 2000 Local Loop Unbundling Regulation. It rests conceptually on the essential-facilities doctrine — though note the US used a "necessary and impair" standard instead. And flag the callout: unbundling creates a brand-new abuse to police, the margin or price squeeze, where the incumbent jacks up the wholesale loop price while dropping its retail price to crush the entrant's margin. Preventing that squeeze is a permanent part of the regulator's job.

Key terms

resale · quasi-facilities · facilities-based · stepping stone / ladder

10.2.1 · FROM SERVICE TO INFRASTRUCTURE COMPETITION

The "ladder of investment".

Unbundling was meant to be a stepping stone. Entrants start cheap (resale), climb as they invest, and eventually build their own infrastructure. Each rung buys more independence from the incumbent — and more sunk cost.

Does the ladder work? Europe says entrants are "climbing"; sceptics (Cambini & Jiang) find LRIC-based unbundling dulls investment by both sides. Evidence is genuinely equivocal — the fight of 10.3.1.
Unbundling was never meant to be the destination — it was meant to be a rung. This is the "ladder of investment", in Europe, or the "stepping-stone" hypothesis in the US, and the diagram lays it out. An entrant starts on the bottom rung as a pure reseller, buying the incumbent's service wholesale and rebranding it, with almost no capital at risk. As it wins customers and confidence it climbs to the middle rung — quasi-facilities competition through local-loop unbundling, investing in its own exchange kit while still renting the loop. The hope is that it keeps climbing to the top rung, full facilities-based competition, where it builds its own end-to-end network and needs the incumbent for nothing. Each step up buys more independence but demands more sunk, unrecoverable investment. Two things to stress. First, the whole regulatory bet is that access at regulated prices bootstraps investment rather than substituting for it. Second — and this is the crux of the empirical fight in 10.3.1 — the evidence is genuinely mixed. European regulators report entrants climbing; sceptics like Cambini and Jiang find that cheap LRIC-based unbundling actually dulls the incentive for both incumbents and entrants to build. Keep that tension live; it decides the fibre debate later.

Key terms

SMP · remedies · common carrier · CPNP · bill & keep

10.2.1 · CABLE, MOBILE & THE SMP FRAMEWORK

Different networks, different regulatory histories.

Cable

Lightly regulated, or not at all. In the US, cable modem = "information service", escaping common-carrier unbundling. Constrained instead by inter-platform competition with the PSTN.

Mobile

Rarely retail-price-regulated. But where CPNP applies (EU, Australia) wholesale termination rates are capped — the "termination monopoly problem". Elsewhere (US, Canada, Singapore): bill & keep.

The SMP test

EU framework, three steps: ① define markets open to ex-ante rules; ② assess who holds significant market power; ③ impose remedies only on the SMP operator.

Modelled on competition law. The EU Regulatory Framework for Electronic Communications borrows its concepts from competition law. But the two can clash: EU courts allow margin-squeeze cases even on regulator-approved rates; US courts (Trinko) largely shield regulated rates from antitrust.
Not every network is regulated like the PSTN, and the differences are instructive. Cable, first, is treated far more lightly — in many countries there's no specific oversight at all. In the US, cable modem service was deliberately classified as an "information service" rather than a "telecommunications service", which spared it the common-carrier unbundling obligations that bind the phone companies; the theory is that cable is disciplined by inter-platform competition wherever its footprint overlaps the PSTN. Mobile, second, is rarely subject to retail price control, but wholesale termination is another matter: where the "calling-party-network-pays" principle applies, as in the EU and Australia, regulators cap mobile termination rates to tackle the termination-monopoly problem — the terminating network is a monopolist over reaching its own subscriber. In bill-and-keep jurisdictions like the US and Singapore, operators simply terminate each other's calls for free. Third, and this is the organising device of European telecoms regulation, the significant-market-power framework: a three-step test — define the market, assess whether anyone holds SMP, and impose remedies only on the SMP holder. It's explicitly modelled on competition law, but the two don't always sit comfortably together — European courts allow margin-squeeze antitrust cases even against regulator-approved rates, whereas the US Trinko decision largely immunises regulated rates from antitrust scrutiny.

Key terms

price cap · RoR · TSLRIC · TELRIC · FL-LRIC · make-or-buy

10.2.2 · FORM & SCOPE OF PRICE CONTROLS

Price caps win — priced off incremental cost.

Price caps have largely displaced rate-of-return regulation (by 2007 only three US states still used RoR). They suit an industry where falling input costs can be passed on without endless rate hearings.

The twist vs other utilities

Elsewhere the revenue requirement uses the "building block" approach. In telecoms it's built on long-run incremental cost — the cost of a hypothetical efficient new entrant, replicating a competitive market.

The alphabet soup

FL-LRIC (Europe), TELRIC (US, Japan — per network element), TSLRIC (Australia, NZ — per service). Aim: recover efficient cost and signal efficient entry.

The hard part. ~80% of BT's costs are joint or common (Newbery). Allocating them across services is unavoidable, contentious, and — done by accounting rules — economically arbitrary. It also shapes the "make-or-buy" decision: price the loop too low and entrants never build.
How are the regulated prices actually set? Two families from Chapter 5 — price caps and rate-of-return — both appear, but price caps have decisively won: by 2007 only three US states still ran rate-of-return regulation. Caps suit telecoms because rapid technological progress keeps cutting input costs like switches and fibre, and a cap lets those savings flow to consumers without a formal rate hearing every year. But there's a telecoms-specific twist worth flagging. In water or energy the revenue requirement is built bottom-up from a "building block" model; in telecoms it's built on long-run incremental cost — deliberately not the incumbent's actual costs but the costs of a hypothetical, maximally efficient new entrant building on a blank slate, the idea being to replicate what a competitive market would deliver. Hence the alphabet soup: forward-looking LRIC in Europe, TELRIC in the US and Japan measured per network element, TSLRIC in Australia and New Zealand measured per service. The genuinely hard part is in the callout: Newbery estimated something like eighty per cent of BT's costs are joint or common across services, and there's no non-arbitrary economic way to split them — you fall back on accounting allocations. And the split isn't cosmetic: set the loop price too low and you distort the entrant's make-or-buy decision, so they rent forever and never build. That single tension drives the next several slides.

Case focus

SAC · DSAC · contestable markets · Ofcom vs BT

BOX 10.1 · PRICE–COST TESTS & CONTESTABLE MARKETS

Are BT's prices "cost-oriented"? Enter the SAC test.

Stand-alone cost as a price ceiling

Contestable-markets theory doesn't fit utilities well (sunk costs make entry threats weak), but two of its cost concepts survive as price bounds. A price is "cost-oriented" if it sits between its incremental cost (floor) and its stand-alone cost (ceiling — what a service would cost if produced alone). The SAC test has policed US rail freight since 1985; Ofcom built a Distributed SAC (DSAC) variant to test BT's trunk and wholesale-Ethernet prices.

The criticisms

DSAC allocates common costs by an equi-proportional accounting rule — "arbitrary" economically. And done properly, the test must cover every combination of services — sometimes over a billion combinations.

The stakes

Highly discretionary, so it invites rent-seeking — "a full employment bill for economists". In 2012 Ofcom ordered BT to repay £94m partly on a DSAC analysis; a US case turned on $345m.

Box 10.1 is a beautiful worked example of theory colliding with practice. In Europe some telecoms prices are no longer capped but must still be "cost-oriented", and in the UK Ofcom operationalised that with a price-cost test drawn from contestable-markets theory. Recall from Chapter 3 that contestable markets don't really describe utilities — sunk costs make the hit-and-run entry threat too weak — but two cost concepts from that literature survive as sanity bounds. A service's price should sit above its incremental cost, the floor, and below its stand-alone cost, the ceiling — the stand-alone cost being what it would cost to produce that service all by itself. The stand-alone-cost test has governed US railroad freight since 1985, and Ofcom built a "distributed stand-alone cost", or DSAC, version to test whether BT's prices for things like trunk access and wholesale Ethernet are cost-oriented. Now the critique, and it's damning. DSAC parcels out common costs using an equi-proportional accounting mark-up that has no real economic meaning. Worse, Faulhaber's original insight was that to apply the test properly you must run it over every possible combination of services — which in practice can mean over a billion combinations. The tests are so discretionary, and the sums so large, that they invite rent-seeking; one judge called the exercise "a full employment bill for economists". Yet the results bite: in 2012 Ofcom ordered BT to repay ninety-four million pounds partly on this basis. The lesson: be wary of leaning too hard on a test this fragile.

10.2.2 · ACCESS PRICING

Fixed loops vs mobile termination vs the internet.

Fixed line (one-way)

Mostly LRIC-based (TSLRIC/TELRIC), occasionally ECPR or retail-minus. Entrant buys access to the incumbent's loop.

Mobile (two-way)

Termination priced at LRIC+. Historically loaded with a network-externality surcharge to fund handset subsidies and boost penetration. The EU now pushes "pure LRIC" — no common-cost mark-up.

Internet (unregulated)

Peering and transit deals, privately negotiated, no regulator. No termination monopoly: if one ISP blocks you, a transit route still reaches the customer.

Why the internet escapes the trap. In circuit-switched networks the terminating operator is a monopolist over its own subscriber — hence rate regulation. On the IP backbone, indirect transit routes mean no single ISP can hold the connection hostage, so private negotiation suffices. As networks converge to IP, which model wins?
Access pricing is where the whole subject gets most contentious, and it splits three ways depending on the network. For fixed lines it's a one-way access problem — the entrant needs the incumbent's loop — and it's mostly priced on LRIC, the TSLRIC or TELRIC approaches, occasionally on the efficient-component-pricing rule or a retail-minus rule. Mobile is a two-way problem, because each operator needs to terminate calls on the other's network, and termination is priced at LRIC-plus — LRIC with a mark-up for common costs. Historically some regulators went further and loaded a network-externality surcharge on top, the reasoning being that operators would recycle the extra termination revenue into handset subsidies that grow the network and benefit everyone — remember the network externality from slide three. The EU has since reversed course toward "pure LRIC" with no common-cost allowance at all. The internet is the fascinating outlier: backbone interconnection runs on privately negotiated peering and transit deals with no regulator in sight. Why can it go unregulated? Because there's no termination monopoly — if one ISP refuses you direct access, you can still reach the end customer indirectly through a transit agreement with a third party, so no one can hold the connection hostage. That's the deep question the box on the next slide, and section 10.3.3, keep circling: as all networks converge to IP, do we regulate them like the old telephone network or leave them like the internet?

BOX 10.2 · BOTTLENECKS & WATERBEDS

Cut the termination rate — and retail prices rise?

The competitive bottleneck

Armstrong (1996) and Laffont–Rey–Tirole (1998): even with fierce retail competition, freely-negotiated access charges can become a collusion device, keeping wholesale prices — and profits — high. So a case for regulation survives competition.

Genakos & Valletti (20 countries). Cutting termination rates ~10% raised mobile retail prices ~5% — a real but incomplete waterbed. The EU & ACCC still doubt it and keep cutting toward LRIC.
Box 10.2 contains two of the most important theoretical results in modern telecoms economics, and they point in opposite directions. First, the competitive-bottleneck insight from Armstrong and from Laffont, Rey and Tirole in the 1990s: in a two-way access setting where operators compete hard for subscribers but must buy termination from each other, the access charges they freely negotiate can actually serve as a collusion device, propping wholesale prices — and profits — up to monopoly levels. The shock was that competition for subscribers does not, by itself, kill the case for regulating wholesale access. That justified capping termination. But then comes the counter-result, the waterbed, and the diagram is the whole idea: this is a two-sided market where wholesale access revenue cross-subsidises the retail side — handset subsidies, cheap calls to win subscribers. So when the regulator presses the wholesale termination rate down, the operator loses that revenue and pushes retail prices back up to recover it. Press the waterbed here, it rises there. Genakos and Valletti tested it across twenty countries and found it real but incomplete — a roughly ten per cent cut in termination rates raised retail prices about five per cent, implying operators keep some termination rent because they have market power. The policy sting: cutting termination rates may not lower prices for consumers overall, which is exactly why the UK Competition Commission believed the waterbed while the EU and Australia kept doubting it and cutting anyway.

10.3.1 · SCOPE & EFFECTS OF RESTRUCTURING

Restructuring worked — but did regulation?

The good news

OECD calls market-opening a "success": prices fell, penetration rose, tariffs rebalanced (cheap long-distance). BT operationally separated its network into Openreach in 2005 after Ofcom found competition inadequate.

The sceptics

Hard to separate policy from raw technology. Hausman & Taylor: below-cost unbundled prices sparked a late-1990s CLEC gold-rush, then mass bankruptcies. "Misguided" regulation cost consumers billions.

The verdict on unbundling. Pindyck: entrants renting in small increments bore no sunk cost — an asymmetric risk split that deterred incumbent investment. Studies increasingly credit US facilities competition to inter-platform rivalry (cable, wireless), not the ladder. Europe's evidence stays equivocal.
So did all this restructuring and regulation actually deliver? The honest answer is: restructuring clearly helped, but the marginal contribution of regulation itself is genuinely disputed. On the positive side, the OECD flatly calls market-opening a success — prices fell, penetration climbed, and tariffs rebalanced so that long-distance and international calls, once kept artificially dear to subsidise local access, became cheap. In the UK, when Ofcom's 2005 strategic review concluded competition was inadequate and unsustainable, BT volunteered to operationally separate its network into Openreach, the arm's-length access provider we still have today. But the sceptics have a serious case. The first problem is attribution: technology was transforming this industry anyway, so how much of the improvement do you credit to regulators versus to Moore's Law? Hausman and Taylor go further — they argue that pricing unbundled elements below cost triggered a speculative gold-rush of competitive local exchange carriers in the late 1990s, followed by a wave of bankruptcies in the early 2000s, and that "misguided" regulatory efforts cost consumers billions. Pindyck's point is the sharpest: entrants could rent network elements in tiny increments for short durations, bearing no sunk cost, which loaded the risk asymmetrically onto the incumbent and blunted its incentive to invest. Increasingly, analysts credit the US shift to facilities-based competition not to the unbundling ladder at all but to inter-platform rivalry from cable and wireless. In Europe the evidence stays stubbornly equivocal.

10.3.2 · THE CONTINUING RATIONALE FOR REGULATION

Is it still a natural monopoly at all?

The historical rationale — PSTN demand and costs look like natural monopoly — may simply no longer hold. Kahn: the industry "is obviously no longer a natural monopoly", and old-style regulation risks being anti-competitive.

The asymmetry problem

Heavily regulate the PSTN incumbent while cable and mobile — offering the same converged services — go lightly regulated? That confers an undue advantage by technology, not by economics.

The proposed fix: granularity

If ex-ante regulation stays, make it geographically granular — regulate only areas where competition is genuinely absent (e.g. rural single-supplier zones), and lean on ex-post competition law elsewhere.

The open question. With IP blurring voice and data, VoIP and mobile substituting for fixed, and inter-platform competition rising, the case for blanket ex-ante price regulation is weaker than ever — but withdrawing it where a bottleneck persists is risky.
Section 10.3.2 asks the most existential question in the chapter: given everything that's changed, is there still a reason to regulate telecoms at all? The historical rationale was that the PSTN's demand and cost characteristics resembled a natural monopoly — but in many jurisdictions that may simply no longer be true. Alfred Kahn, no radical, states it bluntly: the industry is obviously no longer a natural monopoly, and wherever effective platform competition exists — landline, cable, wireless — historical-style regulation is both unnecessary and likely anti-competitive, because it discourages the heavy investment in new platforms we actually want. Two live problems follow. First, asymmetry: if you heavily regulate the PSTN incumbent while cable and mobile deliver the very same converged bundles under light or no regulation, you're handing an advantage based on which technology a firm happens to use, not on any economic bottleneck. Second, the proposed remedy is granularity. Rather than blanket rules, define geographic markets narrowly and impose ex-ante regulation only where competition is genuinely absent — think a rural exchange with a single supplier — while relying on ex-post competition law everywhere competition has arrived. The unresolved tension in the callout: the case for sweeping price regulation is weaker than it's ever been, yet pulling regulation out of the places where the local-loop bottleneck stubbornly persists remains genuinely risky.

10.3.3 · ACCESS & UNBUNDLING FOR NGNs

What do you even unbundle in an all-IP network?

IP interconnection may dissolve the switched-network bottleneck. And engineering changes: the physical hand-off points of copper networks — exchanges, distribution frames, cabinets — can vanish under Fibre-to-the-X.

US · forbearance

Since 2003 the FCC largely forbears from mandatory unbundling on fibre — deliberately, to spur infrastructure investment.

EU · keep the ladder

Access continues via new fibre products — duct, sub-loop, bitstream — and the ladder-of-investment philosophy persists into the NGA world.

And which pricing model? As fixed, mobile and internet converge on IP, cost-based termination charges sit awkwardly beside internet-style peering. The EU and FCC signal a drift away from per-minute cost-based charges toward internet interconnection models.
If we do keep regulating, the shift to all-IP Next-Generation Networks forces us to rethink what unbundling even means. Two distinct issues here — engineering and pricing. On the engineering side: in a copper switched network there are convenient physical hand-off points where a rival can plug in — the exchange, the main distribution frame, the street cabinet. Under Fibre-to-the-X those points can simply cease to exist, so the OECD notes that fibre topologies are much harder, technically and economically, to unbundle. That leaves regulators with a genuine choice, and the two big blocs have gone opposite ways. The US, since the FCC's 2003 decision, largely forbears from mandatory unbundling on new fibre — a deliberate bet that letting incumbents keep their fibre to themselves will spur the huge investment fibre requires. Europe instead preserves unbundling, inventing new fibre-era access products — duct access, sub-loop unbundling, bitstream — and keeping the ladder-of-investment philosophy alive in the NGA environment. On pricing, the convergence pressure is real: as fixed, mobile and internet all become IP, the old cost-based per-minute termination charge sits uneasily next to the internet's peering-and-transit model, and both the European Commission and the FCC are signalling a drift away from cost-based charges toward internet-style interconnection. That transition question is unresolved and consequential.

10.3.4 · INVESTMENT IN NEXT-GENERATION INFRASTRUCTURE

Regulating a network that isn't built yet.

Old regulation shaped networks already sunk. Fibre reverses the problem: rules now shape whether the large, risky investment happens at all. Australia's NBN was costed at A$40bn+; each US fibre-to-the-home passing at $700–800.

Access holiday

Time-limited right to refuse access to new investment (Gans & King). Encourages the risky build.

Forbearance

No fixed time-limit; leaves door open to future regulation. US since 2003.

Risk premium

EU-favoured: add a premium to the cost-of-capital in the access price for investment risk.

Public subsidy

Broadband as public good — state-funded (NBN, Qatar), PPPs, municipal schemes.

The core trade-off. Mandating access delivers short-term price cuts once fibre is built — but the anticipation of that access can kill the incentive to build it in the first place. That tension is the fibre debate.
Section 10.3.4 is where the whole chapter's tension crystallises around fibre. Notice the fundamental change in the regulator's problem: for thirty years regulation dealt with networks that were already built and whose costs were already sunk — the job was dividing up the spoils. Fibre inverts that. Now regulation has to shape whether an enormous, genuinely risky investment gets made at all. The numbers are sobering — Australia's National Broadband Network was costed north of forty billion Australian dollars, and each fibre-to-the-home connection in the US runs seven to eight hundred dollars per premises passed. The trade-off in the callout is the heart of it: if you mandate that the incumbent share its new fibre at regulated prices, consumers get cheaper access once it's built — but the mere anticipation of forced sharing can destroy the incentive to build in the first place, because rivals reap the upside without bearing the risk. Four proposed fixes. An access holiday, from Gans and King, is a time-limited right to refuse access to new investment. Forbearance is the open-ended version with no fixed clock. The EU prefers a risk premium — bumping the cost-of-capital in the access price to compensate for investment risk. And where the market won't deliver, public subsidy treats broadband as a public good, funded through vehicles like the NBN, public-private partnerships or municipal schemes. Hold the holiday idea — the next slide is the case study of it blowing up in Europe.

BOX 10.3 · REGULATORY HOLIDAYS IN EUROPE

Germany tried a holiday. The court said no.

The Deutsche Telekom VDSL case

Gans & King (2003) proposed the "regulatory holiday" — commit not to apply ex-ante access regulation to new investment automatically, only later and only if conditions are met. In 2007 Germany amended its Telecommunications Act to exempt Deutsche Telekom's new VDSL network from wholesale-access obligations. The European Commission challenged it as incompatible with the common framework, and the European Court of Justice ultimately deemed it unlawful — DT was already dominant in broadband.

Still a live wire

The Netherlands kept trying to exempt KPN's fibre (2012); Brazil committed to a nine-year holiday to 2021. The idea won't die.

The Commission's line

"Any suggestion of regulatory forbearance in Europe has been soundly dismissed." The Digital Agenda goals are to be met by the standard regulatory approach — not holidays.

Box 10.3 turns the abstract holiday debate into a concrete legal showdown. The regulatory holiday, proposed by Gans and King in 2003, is a commitment by the regulator not to apply ex-ante access regulation to new investment automatically — only later, and only once specified conditions are met — so that the investor gets a window to earn a return before rivals can demand access. Germany actually tried it: in 2007 it amended its Telecommunications Act to carve out "new markets", which would have exempted Deutsche Telekom's new high-speed VDSL network from any obligation to provide wholesale access to competitors. The European Commission objected that this was incompatible with the common European regulatory framework — the sticking point being that Deutsche Telekom was already dominant in broadband, so a holiday would entrench that dominance — and the European Court of Justice ultimately struck the exemption down as unlawful. But notice the idea refuses to die: the Netherlands kept trying to exempt KPN's fibre network in 2012, and Brazil, which borrows heavily from the European framework, committed to a nine-year holiday running to 2021. The Commission's own position could not be blunter — one OECD line records that any suggestion of regulatory forbearance in Europe "has been soundly dismissed", and the Commission insists the Digital Agenda's broadband targets will be met by standard regulation, not holidays. It's the clearest live example of the investment-versus-access trade-off playing out in court.

10.3.5 · NET NEUTRALITY

Should every packet be treated equally?

Neutrality supporters

Google, Facebook, civil-liberty groups. Without rules, a vertically-integrated ISP has an incentive to throttle or block rivals — degrade Skype to push its own voice, or Netflix/BitTorrent to push its own TV. Non-discrimination "subsidised" the internet's innovation.

Prioritisation defenders

Absent market power, letting ISPs charge for quality (a "fast lane") is welfare-enhancing price differentiation — and funds network investment. Congestion-sensitive services become viable.

The FCC saga. 2010 Order: disclose practices, no blocking of lawful content, no unreasonable discrimination. Struck down by the DC Circuit in Jan 2014, then re-proposed. The fear opponents raise: "specialised services" degrade the free best-efforts internet until content providers are forced to buy the fast lane.
Net neutrality is the newest and most politically charged issue in the chapter, and it's really a question about traffic management: should ISPs treat every packet equally, or may they speed up, slow down, block or charge for particular traffic? The internet historically ran on a best-efforts basis — all content delivered on equal terms. The neutrality camp — Google, Facebook, civil-liberties groups — worries that a vertically-integrated ISP has an incentive to discriminate against services that compete with its own: a phone company throttling Skype to protect its voice revenue, a cable operator degrading Netflix or BitTorrent to protect its TV. Their deeper claim is that non-discrimination effectively subsidised content creation and drove the internet's extraordinary innovation, and that abandoning it risks a fragmented internet where you can only reach content your ISP has cut a deal with. The other camp argues that, absent genuine market power, letting ISPs charge for higher quality — a fast lane — is just ordinary, welfare-enhancing price differentiation that also funds the network investment we badly need, and makes congestion-sensitive services viable. The regulatory story is the FCC saga: its 2010 Order required disclosure, banned blocking of lawful content, and barred unreasonable discrimination; a court struck the core rules down in January 2014, and the FCC re-proposed them. The sharpest opponent argument — flagged by Economides — is that ISPs could deliberately let the free best-efforts internet rot until content providers are coerced into buying the premium "specialised service", so the fast lane becomes a backdoor toll on the whole internet.

10.3.6 · THE SCOPE OF UNIVERSAL SERVICE

Is broadband now a universal right?

Universal service obligations historically guaranteed everyone a voice line at an affordable, geographically-uniform price — funded by cross-subsidy. The live question: extend it to broadband internet?

Why it matters now

Fibre is being built mainly in dense urban areas — risking a digital divide or "broadband gap" between town and country. Noam: lack of connectivity retards participation in the economy, politics and culture.

Where it stands

EU: "functional internet access" required since 2002 (mostly narrowband so far). US National Broadband Plan (2010): ≥4 Mbps down / 1 Mbps up for all. Finland, Spain, Switzerland & others have folded broadband into their USO.

The economics. A uniform tariff across a region ignores that rural supply costs far more than urban — the classic cross-subsidy problem. Extending the USO to fibre broadband makes that cost gap larger and the funding question sharper.
The last policy issue, 10.3.6, is universal service — and it's the oldest idea in the chapter meeting the newest technology. Historically, universal service obligations guaranteed every household a basic voice telephone line at an affordable, geographically-uniform price, regardless of the fact that serving a remote farm costs far more than serving a city flat; the gap was papered over with cross-subsidy, typically long-distance and business users subsidising local and residential ones. The live question is whether that guarantee should now extend to broadband internet. Why it's urgent: fibre investment naturally concentrates in dense urban areas where the economics work, which threatens a digital divide — a broadband gap between town and country — and, as Noam argues, lack of connectivity increasingly locks people out of economic, political and cultural life, making broadband feel like a political entitlement. Where does it stand? The EU has required "functional internet access" since 2002, though in practice that's often meant only narrowband so far. The US 2010 National Broadband Plan set a target of at least four megabits down and one up for every household. And several countries — Finland, Spain, Switzerland, Malta, Israel — have already folded broadband into their formal universal-service obligations. The economic sting in the callout is the familiar one: a uniform tariff ignores the real cost difference between rural and urban supply, and extending the obligation to expensive fibre only widens that gap and sharpens the question of who pays.
