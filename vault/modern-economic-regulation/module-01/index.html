CHAPTER 1 · ISSUES OF TERMINOLOGY

Four words to pin down before we start.

Different countries use different labels for the same things. Decker fixes a house vocabulary — worth learning now, because it recurs in every later chapter.

Public utility industries

His umbrella term for electricity, gas, water & wastewater, transport and telecoms — where others say "utilities" or "network industries". Telecoms is included, because most regulators cover it.

Core network

The activities in a supply chain with natural-monopoly attributes — pipes, wires, rails and poles. Replaces the older habit of calling a whole utility a "natural monopoly".

Restructuring policy

1980s–90s reforms that removed entry barriers, mandated non-discriminatory access to the core network, and/or forced vertical & horizontal separation. He avoids "liberalisation" and "deregulation".

Jurisdiction

An autonomous geographic area — a US state, an EU Member State — not "country". States within one country often regulate very differently.

Chapter 1 opens with terminology, and it's worth taking seriously because the words are doing analytical work, not just tidying. "Public utility industries" is his umbrella for the five sectors; note he deliberately includes telecoms, even though some traditions exclude it, because in practice most regulators have it in their remit. "Core network" is the important one. Older texts called an entire utility a "natural monopoly", but after restructuring that's wrong — you can compete in generation or retail even while the wires stay a monopoly. So "core network" names just the monopoly transport layer: pipes, wires, rails, poles. He also picks "restructuring" over "liberalisation" and "deregulation" on purpose — "liberalisation" carries ideological baggage, and "deregulation" is simply inaccurate, because regulation wasn't withdrawn, it was replaced with different forms of oversight. Finally "jurisdiction" rather than "country", because a US state or an EU Member State is the real unit of regulatory choice, and they diverge sharply. Learn these four now; they're load-bearing for the whole book.

Key terms

structure · conduct · social vs economic regulation

CHAPTER 1 · WHAT IS ECONOMIC REGULATION?

Broader than price control — structure and conduct.

Decker's "economic regulation" = state interventions that shape the structure of an industry or guide the conduct of its firms.

Structure

How many firms may supply a service; requiring separate entities for different supply-chain activities; requiring third-party access to infrastructure.

Conduct

Firms' decisions on pricing, investment, quality, coverage, and the terms on which competitors get access.

The deliberately blurred edge. He rejects a sharp "economic vs social regulation" line for the utilities. You can't regulate electricity without environmental subsidies, or gas/water without health & safety, or telecoms without universal-service ("broadband for all") duties. So economic regulation here also captures interventions steering firms toward wider social & environmental goals — usually via financial incentives.
This is objective one, and the point that surprises people. Ask a practitioner what economic regulation is and they'll say "price controls". Decker's definition is much wider on two axes. On structure, it covers how many firms may operate, whether activities must be legally separated, and whether incumbents must grant access to their infrastructure. On conduct, it covers pricing yes, but also investment, quality, coverage, and access terms for rivals. Then comes the move that matters: he refuses to draw the usual firewall between "economic" regulation and "social" regulation. For the utilities that firewall is untenable. You genuinely cannot discuss electricity or water regulation without environmental policy, because subsidies and incentives for clean generation flow straight through the price control. You can't discuss gas or water without health and safety, because safety standards drive network costs. And telecoms has always carried social-inclusion duties — universal service, now "broadband for all". So his economic regulation deliberately includes measures that push firms toward social and environmental objectives, typically through financial incentives. Hold that breadth: it's why later chapters keep touching policy you might not expect.

Key terms

ex ante · ex post · unbundling · one-way / two-way access

CHAPTER 1 · THE TWO SPINE DISTINCTIONS

Ring-fence the monopoly; free the rest.

The central design idea of modern regulation: unbundle a vertically-integrated utility into a monopoly core network and the potentially competitive activities around it. Regulate the core tightly, up front (ex ante); let competition — policed after the fact by competition law (ex post) — run in the rest.

Ex ante vs ex post. Write the rules before the firm acts (a dedicated regulator, price caps, access terms), or challenge conduct afterwards (antitrust). Modern utilities use both — the balance is a recurring theme.
This slide holds the two distinctions that form the spine of the entire book — I've drawn them because Chapter 1 states them in prose. First, the core-network idea made concrete. Restructuring "unbundled" the old vertically-integrated utility: the diagram shows a monopoly core network in the middle — the pipes, wires, rails and poles — with potentially competitive activities on either side, generation or production upstream and retail or supply downstream. The regulatory strategy follows the shape: bear down hard on the core, where a single firm is genuinely cheapest and competition can't discipline it, while letting rivals compete in the surrounding layers. That's why "access" matters — competitors must be let onto the core network on non-discriminatory terms, and pricing that access is a whole chapter later. Second distinction, the ex-ante versus ex-post choice: do you write the rules before the firm acts, which is what a dedicated regulator with price caps does, or do you challenge bad conduct afterwards through competition law? Modern utility regulation uses both, and how they interact — especially where networks start competing — is a theme Decker flags for later.

Scale of the stakes

≈ 7.2% of a US household's spend · 4–6% of GDP in developed economies

CHAPTER 1 · WHY THE PUBLIC UTILITIES?

Essential, unavoidable — and often monopoly-supplied.

Imagine a prolonged blackout, a gas outage, water contamination or a rail shutdown — the stakes are obvious. We consume these services daily, directly and embedded in everything else. A four-person US household spent about $4,500 a year (7.2% of spending) on utilities in 2010; the sector is 4–6% of GDP across developed economies.

Two forces combine. Services that are essential and ubiquitous, supplied by firms in strong or monopoly positions — sometimes for natural cost reasons, sometimes by policy. Left unregulated, such firms may raise prices, cut quality, or under-invest.
This is Decker's Figure 1.1, and objective two. Don't fixate on the individual bars — the teaching point is the "Total" pair on the right and the direction of travel: a four-person US household spent roughly $4,500 a year on utilities in 2010, about 7.2% of total spending, and it rose between 2007 and 2010. Electricity is the largest single line and grew most; telephone is close behind. Beyond the household, these industries are 4 to 6% of GDP in developed economies, and they're embedded in everything else we buy. That unavoidability is doing real moral and political work. If gas were a luxury, a gouging monopolist would be a nuisance; because it's essential, it becomes a public-policy problem. And that's exactly Decker's argument for singling these industries out: essential, ubiquitous services are frequently supplied by firms holding strong or outright monopoly positions — sometimes because the cost structure makes that natural, sometimes because policy created it. Combine "essential" with "monopoly" and you get a standing worry that, unregulated, the firm raises prices, lets quality slide, or under-invests. That combination is the reason the whole field exists.

The paradox

Principles ≈ 130 years old · context transformed in the last 30

1.2 · WHAT IS 'MODERN' ABOUT IT?

Old subject, re-made in 1984.

US regulatory commissions date to the late 1880s; classic texts run from Bonbright (1961) to Kahn (1971). So what's new? Decker dates the modern era to two events, both 1984, exactly three decades before he wrote.

  • 1880s First US regulatory commissions; a century of rate-setting scholarship begins.
  • 1984 · USA Court-endorsed break-up of AT&T (Ch. 10). Incumbent regional monopolies now coexist with competitors — creating the access & interconnection problem that defines modern regulation.
  • 1984 · Britain RPI–X price caps in newly restructured telecoms (Ch. 5), replacing US-style rate-of-return regulation — the template copied worldwide, alongside new independent regulators.
  • 1990s→ Restructuring spreads: USA, EU Member States, UK (a pioneer), Chile, Argentina, Australia, New Zealand — and, increasingly, developing economies.
    • The surprise. Early-1980s forecasts said regulation would wither as competition took hold. Instead regulators' remits — and often their size — expanded.
    Objective three: what earns the word "modern" for a subject this old? Decker is candid — regulating utilities is not new. The first US commissions appear in the late 1880s, and the intellectual canon runs from Bonbright's 1961 rates book through Kahn's 1971 Economics of Regulation. Many core principles, especially for the monopoly core network, are as valid now as when they were framed. What changed is the context, and he anchors it to two events in 1984, exactly thirty years before publication. In the United States, the court-endorsed break-up of AT&T shattered a vertically integrated telecoms monopoly into a structure where regional incumbents coexisted with competitors — which is precisely what created the access and interconnection question that dominates modern regulation. In Britain, the newly restructured telecoms industry adopted RPI-minus-X price caps instead of American-style rate-of-return regulation, and that innovation, together with the new independent regulatory offices, became the template copied across the world. The paradox to leave them with: many economists in the early 1980s expected regulation to fade as competition arrived. The opposite happened. Splitting industries into competitive and monopoly parts spawned brand-new problems, so regulators' remits grew.

    Three drivers

    technology (ICT) · environment · a broadened remit

    1.2 · WHY THE REGULATOR'S JOB GREW

    Three forces widened the remit.

    ICT & "smart" networks

    New telecoms platforms — cable, fibre, mobile — and near-real-time trading in gas & electricity, plus smart meters feeding consumption signals to end-users.

    Environment & society

    Shift from polluting to low-carbon generation; restricting (or encouraging) shale gas; water sustainability — extraction, trading, treatment.

    A broadened remit

    Beyond price & quality: manage the transition to competition, get consumers to engage, design tariffs to cut some demand and boost others.

    Tariffs pulling both ways. The same regulator may design prices to reduce electricity and water use for environmental reasons, yet increase mobile take-up to harness positive network externalities. And regulatory agencies now exist across the world — no longer just the US, UK and other rich economies.
    This slide fleshes out why the remit expanded rather than contracted — Decker names three forces. First, information and communications technology. In telecoms it produced whole new platforms — cable, fibre, mobile — that can each carry the same services, so "the network" is no longer one thing. But ICT also reshaped the other utilities: near-real-time trading in gas and electricity, and smart networks and meters that push live information to end-users and can change how much they consume. Second, environmental and social concern. This drove policies shifting electricity away from heavy-polluting generation toward renewables and low-carbon sources, policies encouraging or restricting unconventional gas like shale, and water sustainability rules on abstraction, trading and treatment. Third, the remit itself broadened: a modern regulator is no longer just setting prices and policing quality — it manages the structural transition from monopoly to competition, works to get consumers actively engaging in the market, and designs tariff structures with opposite aims in different sectors. Note that lovely tension in the callout: the same toolkit is used to suppress electricity and water demand on green grounds, yet to stimulate mobile demand to capture network externalities.

    1.3 · NEW EXPERIENCE AND ANALYSIS

    New questions layered on enduring ones.

    Modern regulation mixes old puzzles — how to incentivise efficiency, handle asymmetric information, choose an industry structure — with new ones thrown up by competition inside formerly monopoly industries.

    The access problem

    One-way access: a single core-network provider supplies an essential input to firms in related activities. Two-way access: providers both sell inputs to, and buy inputs from, each other. Pricing this is central and new.

    Structure & coordination

    With separate entities now investing and operating, how do you coordinate investment? Fuels the vertical integration vs separation debate.

    Ex ante meets ex post

    "Infrastructure competition" between rival networks raises the question of how up-front regulation should interact with reliance on competition / antitrust law.

    Judging the experiments

    Restructuring is now being assessed (Chs 8–11): benefits to households sometimes unclear; "consumers can't represent themselves" no longer self-evident; poor fit of one-size-fits-all reform in developing economies.

    Section 1.3 is Decker's preview of the analytical agenda, and it's where the modern questions get named. His framing: modern regulation is a mix of new and old. The enduring puzzles never went away — how to incentivise a regulated firm to be efficient, how to cope with the fact that the firm knows its own costs far better than the regulator, which is the asymmetric-information problem, and which industry structure to prefer. What competition inside formerly monopoly industries added is, above all, the access problem. Distinguish the two flavours he introduces: one-way access, where a single core-network provider supplies an essential input to firms operating in related downstream activities; and two-way access, where providers simultaneously sell inputs to and buy inputs from one another. Pricing access well is genuinely new and gets its own chapter. Separation also created a coordination problem — when different entities invest and operate, who ensures the investments line up? — which fuels the vertical integration versus separation debate. And crucially, the experiments are now old enough to assess. Some findings are uncomfortable: household benefits are sometimes hard to see, the old assumption that consumers can't represent themselves looks shakier, and one-size-fits-all reform fitted developing economies badly.

    Why it's the emblem

    Created the coexistence of monopoly & competition — the modern condition.

    CASE STUDY · THE MODERN ERA'S FOUNDING EVENT

    1984 · The AT&T break-up.

    What happened, and why it still matters.

    In 1984 a US court endorsed the separation of the incumbent telecoms giant AT&T. Overnight the industry's structure changed: regional monopoly firms now coexisted with potentially competitive firms. That single structural fact generated the defining questions of modern regulation — on what terms should the incumbent give competitors access and interconnection to its network, and at what price?

    Pair it with Britain, 1984

    Same year, Britain's restructured telecoms adopted RPI–X price caps and set up an independent regulator — the price-control template the world then copied.

    The through-line to this book

    Both events created the monopoly-plus-competition condition. Access pricing (Ch. 6), price-cap design (Ch. 5), and the telecoms case (Ch. 10) all trace back here.

    Think about it. Before 1984, a single integrated firm owned network and services, so "access terms" were an internal matter. Why does splitting them turn access into a regulatory problem — and why can't ordinary contract law always solve it?
    Decker doesn't run a formal box in Chapter 1, but the AT&T break-up is the chapter's emblematic case, so I've built it out. The facts are simple: in 1984 a US court endorsed separating AT&T, the incumbent telecoms giant. What matters is the structural consequence — regional monopoly firms now had to coexist with competitors. That coexistence is the modern condition, and it immediately raises the questions that occupy much of the book: on what terms, and at what price, must the incumbent grant competitors access and interconnection to its network? Pair it deliberately with the British event of the same year — RPI-minus-X price caps plus an independent regulator — and you have the two halves of "modern": a new structure in the US, a new price-control instrument in the UK, both from 1984, both copied globally. Use the exercise to make the access point bite. Before the break-up, network and services sat inside one firm, so access was an internal accounting matter. Split them and access becomes a bargaining relationship between an essential-facility owner and firms that depend on it — and because the owner also competes downstream, ordinary contract law can't be trusted to produce fair terms. That's why you need a regulator.

    1.4 · STRUCTURE OF THE BOOK

    Three Parts: why, how-in-theory, how-in-practice.

    The whole book — and this course — is organised in three movements. Part I asks why we regulate and what the alternatives are; Part II distils the analytical principles; Part III turns to institutions and the four industries in practice, then concludes.

    Where the modules go. Ch. 2–3 (Part I), Ch. 4–6 (Part II), Ch. 7–12 (Part III). Every later module in this course sits in one of these three boxes.
    Section 1.4 lays out the book's architecture, and it's worth memorising because it is also the spine of this course. Three Parts. Part I is about the "why": Chapter 2 asks why the utilities are regulated at all, splitting normative explanations — why we should regulate — from more positive, interest-group explanations of why we actually do; Chapter 3 then surveys the alternatives to standard regulation, including competition "for the market" via franchising, state ownership, negotiated settlements, and reliance on ex-post competition law. Part II is the "how" in theory, drawing on analytical work: Chapter 4 sets out the general principles for the monopoly core network under different information and product assumptions; Chapter 5 covers the actual forms of price regulation — rate-of-return, price caps, yardstick and benchmarking; Chapter 6 tackles access pricing, one-way and two-way. Part III is the "how" in practice: Chapter 7 on institutions and the independent regulator, Chapters 8 to 11 walking through electricity, gas, telecoms and water in turn with evidence and case boxes, and Chapter 12 drawing conclusions and future challenges.     CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

    What does Decker mean by the "core network"?

    A
    The entire vertically-integrated public utility — generation, transport and retail together.

    No — that's the old usage he explicitly rejects. Restructuring unbundled the utility, so calling the whole thing a natural monopoly is no longer apt.

    B
    Only the activities with natural-monopoly attributes — the transport layer: pipes, wires, rails and poles.

    ✓ Correct. The core network is the ring-fenced monopoly transport element; competitive layers sit around it (Fig 1.2).

    C
    The set of independent regulatory agencies coordinating across jurisdictions.

    False — that confuses "core network" (an industry structure term) with regulatory institutions, the subject of Chapter 7.

    Quick self-check on the chapter's single most important term. The trap is answer A — the older, pre-restructuring habit of calling an entire vertically-integrated utility a "natural monopoly". Decker rejects that precisely because unbundling separated the monopoly bits from the competitive bits, so the label no longer fits the whole. The correct answer is B: the core network is only the transport layer with natural-monopoly attributes — the pipes, wires, rails and poles — ring-fenced and regulated tightly, with competitive activities like generation and retail arranged around it, exactly as we drew in Figure 1.2. C is a distractor that swaps the structural concept for the institutional one — independent agencies are real and important, but they're the subject of Chapter 7, not what "core network" means. If a learner picks A, send them back to the terminology slide; if C, back to the unbundling diagram. The goal is that they can not just recite "core network" but explain why the term had to change once the industries were restructured.
