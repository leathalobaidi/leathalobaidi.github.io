Welcome to Module 4 — the analytical heart of the course. Chapters 1 to 3 asked why we regulate and what the alternatives are. From here on the question changes to the "how". This chapter deals only with the core network activities — the bits of the production chain that most resemble natural monopoly and where competition is not an option. Almost everything in the chapter is about pricing, and the storyline is a ladder of increasingly realistic assumptions. We start in a fantasy world of perfect information and a single product, where the answer is elegant and clean. SECTION 4.1

Where does the
core network sit?

Before you can price it, you have to locate it. Three generic industry structures determine whether the regulator is setting retail prices, access prices, or policing an integrated firm that is both monopolist and competitor.

A short divider to reset attention. The reason Decker opens with industry structure is that the regulatory task — and therefore the relevant pricing principles — depends entirely on the shape of the industry. In a fully integrated monopoly the regulator sets final retail prices and never touches access. In a vertically separated industry, the monopoly network is ring-fenced and the regulator sets access prices for the competitive firms upstream and downstream. In the trickiest case, integration with competition, the network owner also competes in the open markets, so the regulator must both regulate the network and stop the firm leveraging its monopoly. This chapter deals only with the core network activity itself — natural-monopoly pricing. The access-pricing questions that arise in structures two and three are deferred to Chapter 6. But you need the map first, so let's draw all three.

Key terms

vertical integration · legal monopoly · retail price regulation

4.1 · STRUCTURE ①

One firm does everything.

A single firm — usually a legal monopoly — runs the whole chain: production, transport and retail. Common across Europe, Australia and New Zealand before the 1980s–90s. England & Wales water and wastewater still looks like this.

Regulator's job. Pure natural-monopoly regulation of the final retail price. No access pricing — there are no rivals needing to plug in.
This is Decker's Figure 4.1. One firm sits astride the entire production chain — the upstream activity that makes the product, the core network that transports it, and the downstream activity that retails it — and it typically holds a legal monopoly protecting it from entry at every level. This was the default structure for utilities across most of Europe and the Commonwealth until the restructuring wave. The live UK example to remember is water and wastewater in England and Wales: unlike energy and telecoms, it was never vertically unbundled, so it still looks essentially like this diagram. Implicit in keeping this structure is a judgement that competition isn't feasible or worthwhile anywhere in the chain, or that the efficiencies of integration are worth protecting. For the regulator the task is simple in principle: apply natural-monopoly pricing to the final retail price. Crucially there is no access-pricing problem here — access pricing only arises once you let competitors into one layer of the chain, which is exactly the next two structures.

Key terms

unbundling · one-way access · access price regulation

4.1 · STRUCTURE ②

Ring-fence the pipe; compete around it.

Separate the naturally-monopolistic core (transmission & distribution) from the potentially competitive layers (generation, retail). The network firm operates only the network. This is gas and electricity in many jurisdictions.

Two regulatory jobs. Natural-monopoly pricing of the core network plus the terms and conditions of one-way access for competitors plugging in above and below.
This is Figure 4.2, the structure that restructuring created. The insight of the 1980s–90s reforms was that a utility is not one indivisible monopoly — it's a chain in which only some links are genuinely natural monopolies. In electricity and gas, transmission and distribution — the wires and pipes — have natural-monopoly characteristics, but generation and retail supply do not. So you separate them: the network firm runs only the network and is barred from operating in the competitive markets around it. Multiple generators feed into the shared network at the top; multiple retailers draw from it at the bottom; consumers choose their retailer. Now the regulator has two jobs, not one. It still applies natural-monopoly pricing to the core network. But it must also govern the terms of one-way access — the price and conditions on which competitors plug into the monopoly pipe. Get that access price wrong and you either choke off competition or let the network owner extract a monopoly toll. Those access-pricing principles are the subject of Chapter 6; this chapter stays focused on pricing the network itself.

Key terms

local loop · leverage · competitor-and-customer

4.1 · STRUCTURE ③

The awkward one: monopolist and rival.

The network owner also competes in the open markets. Classic telecoms: the former incumbent owns the local loop and sells retail services. Entrants must buy access to the loop from the very firm they compete with — both customer and competitor.

The extra hazard. The firm can leverage its network monopoly into the competitive layer — margin squeeze, discrimination. Regulation must price the network and police that leverage.
This is Figure 4.3, and it is the structure that gives regulators the biggest headache — the telecoms case above all. Here the network owner has not been separated out. The former incumbent still owns the local loop, which is the natural-monopoly bottleneck, but it also sells retail services in the competitive markets. The dashed box is the incumbent's own retail arm sitting alongside independent entrants. Those entrants face an uncomfortable dependency: to sell to consumers they must buy access to the loop from the incumbent, so they are simultaneously the incumbent's customer for the input and its competitor in the retail market. That dual relationship is the crux. The firm has both the ability and the incentive to leverage its bottleneck monopoly into the competitive layer — by setting a high access price and a low retail price it can squeeze the entrants' margins, or degrade the quality of access it gives rivals. So regulation here is doubled: price the core network on natural-monopoly principles, and separately police the firm's conduct to stop it leaning on its monopoly. Note that our chapter treats only the first job; the leverage question is Chapter 6.

SECTION 4.2

The clean world:
full information, one product

Assume the regulator knows everything — costs, cost-minimising effort, demand — the firm makes one product, and there is only one period. In this fantasy the efficient price is obvious. Then reality intrudes: fixed costs.

We now start the pricing ladder, and the first rung is deliberately unrealistic. Assume the regulator has perfect information about the firm's costs — including how hard it is trying to minimise them — and about demand. Assume the firm produces a single product, and everything happens in one period, a static setting. These assumptions are false, and we'll relax them one by one, but they give us the clean benchmark against which every messier result is judged. Two vocabulary distinctions to bank before we start, because they run through the next several slides. First, uniform versus discriminatory pricing: one price for everybody, versus different prices for different customer classes by willingness to pay. Second, linear versus non-linear pricing: does the price per unit stay constant as you consume more, or does the average price change with quantity? Standing charges plus per-unit charges are the everyday non-linear example. Hold those four words — you'll need them.

Key terms

first-best · allocative efficiency · SRMC vs LRMC

4.2.1 · THE STARTING PRINCIPLE

Price = marginal cost. Full stop.

Kahn: "The central policy prescription of microeconomics is the equation of price and marginal cost." Set P = MC and total surplus is maximised — every unit worth more than it costs gets made, and no unit that costs more than it's worth. This is first-best.

Why it's optimal

Any deviation — above or below MC — destroys surplus. Traces back to Dupuit (1840s) and Hotelling (1938).

Two practical snags

Which MC — short-run or long-run? And what's the "unit"? The answers decide which costs count as fixed vs variable.

The second-best caveat. First-best only holds if applied everywhere. Price one product at MC while its substitute is unconstrained and you distort choices — the theorem of the second best (Lipsey–Lancaster 1956).
Marginal-cost pricing is the benchmark of efficiency and the point at which the whole inquiry begins — that's Kahn's line, worth quoting verbatim. The logic is the one from Module 1: at price equals marginal cost, every trade that is worth more to a consumer than it costs society to produce actually happens, and none that isn't. Deviate in either direction and you lose surplus — price above MC excludes willing buyers, price below MC induces wasteful consumption. That's why it's called first-best. The idea is old, traceable to the French engineer Dupuit in the 1840s and developed by Hotelling in 1938, who argued you should price at marginal cost even in decreasing-cost industries and cover the deficit from general taxation — a claim that ignited the "marginal cost controversy". Flag two caveats. Practically, you have to specify which marginal cost — short-run or long-run — and what increment you're costing, and those choices determine what's fixed and what's variable. Theoretically, the second-best theorem: marginal-cost pricing is only genuinely first-best if it's applied uniformly. Price one good at MC and leave its substitute unconstrained and you've just introduced a new distortion.

The cost function

TC(Q) = cQ + F
price = MC = c ⟹ fixed cost F unrecovered

4.2.2 · WHY FIRST-BEST FAILS HERE

Perfect pricing bankrupts the network.

Core networks are nearly all fixed cost (F) and very little variable cost (c). Set P = MC = c and revenue is cQ — it never touches F. The firm makes a loss on every unit and supply becomes unsustainable.

Option A · Transfer subsidy

Government tops up the shortfall = F. But funding it distorts the wider economy, non-users subsidise users, and most independent regulators are barred from subsidising (capture risk).

Option B · Deviate from MC

Let prices rise above MC — but cleverly — to recover F with minimum surplus loss. The rest of §4.2–4.3 is variations on this idea.

The core conflict. Allocative efficiency (P=MC) vs firm viability (revenue ≥ TC). Economies of scale mean the two can't both hold. Every pricing rule that follows is a compromise.
This is the hinge of the whole chapter, so slow down. Core network activities have a cost function that looks like total cost equals c times Q plus F — a small constant marginal cost c per unit, and a large fixed cost F that doesn't vary with output. Because of economies of scale, average cost is falling and always sits above marginal cost. Now apply the beautiful first-best rule: set price equal to marginal cost, which here is just c. Revenue is c times Q. But total cost is cQ plus F. So the firm's revenue falls short of its costs by exactly F, the fixed cost, on every unit and in aggregate. Marginal-cost pricing bankrupts the utility. There are only two families of fix. Option A is a transfer subsidy — the state simply hands the firm F. Hotelling loved this; Coase savaged it. The problems are that raising the money distorts the economy, non-users end up subsidising users, and critically most independent regulators are legally forbidden from paying subsidies precisely because it opens the door to capture. So in practice we're pushed to Option B: allow prices to deviate above marginal cost, but do it as cleverly as possible to recover F while sacrificing the least surplus. Everything from here to the end of section 4.3 is a different clever answer to that one question.

Welfare function

V(P) = CS(P) + α·π(P)
max s.t. π ≥ 0 ⟹ P* = AC

4.2.3 · THE SECOND-BEST

Charge average cost — the least-bad single price.

Maximise welfare subject to break-even and the answer is a single uniform price at average cost (P* = AC). The firm makes zero profit, the price is as low as break-even allows, and consumer surplus is maximised.

But it's sub-optimal. At P*=AC output is Q*, short of the first-best Q′. Consumers who'd have paid MC but not AC are shut out — the shaded welfare loss.
This is Figure 4.4, the second-best result, and the first honest compromise. Write the regulator's problem as maximising a welfare function V — a weighted sum of consumer surplus and profit, where alpha between zero and one lets you weight profit less than consumer surplus — subject to the firm making non-negative profit. Solve it and the answer is elegant: set a single uniform price equal to average cost. At that price the firm exactly breaks even, and because break-even is binding, average cost is the lowest uniform price that keeps the firm alive, which is why it maximises consumer surplus. If you set price above AC you could cut it and raise welfare; below AC and the firm can't survive. So AC pricing is the best single price you can charge. But look at what it costs you on the diagram. First-best output is Q-prime, where demand meets the low marginal-cost curve. Average-cost pricing only gets you to Q-star, a smaller output at the higher price P-star equals AC. The consumers sitting between Q-star and Q-prime — the ones who'd happily pay marginal cost, and even a bit above, but not the full average cost — are excluded. That shaded triangle is the allocative inefficiency, the welfare loss, and it's the price of keeping the firm solvent with one uniform price. Every subsequent rule tries to shrink that triangle.

Inverse elasticity rule

(Pᵢ − Cᵢ)/Pᵢ = λ/ηᵢ
mark-up ∝ 1 / elasticity

4.2.4 & 4.3.2 · RAMSEY–BOITEUX PRICING

Load the mark-up onto the captive customer.

Don't spread the fixed-cost mark-up evenly. Make each product's (or group's) mark-up above MC inversely proportional to its demand elasticity. Inelastic services carry a big mark-up; elastic services price near MC. This minimises the surplus lost while still recovering F.

Single product

Different customer groups get different mark-ups by elasticity — resembles a monopolist's third-degree discrimination, but at a lower price level.

Multi-product

Each product's mark-up ∝ 1/ηᵢ. "What the traffic will bear" / value-of-service pricing. λ (the Ramsey number) is tuned so π = 0.

Cross-price effects

With substitutes/complements use "super-elasticities": smaller mark-ups for complements, larger for substitutes.

The unifying principle. The inverse-elasticity rule underlies all efficient pricing — it even generates optimal non-linear tariffs by treating each consumption increment as a separate "market".
Ramsey–Boiteux pricing is the most beautiful idea in the chapter and, as we'll see, the one regulators most stubbornly refuse to use. The insight: average-cost pricing spreads the fixed-cost mark-up evenly across all units, which is arbitrary. You can do better. Instead, make the mark-up above marginal cost on each product — or each customer group — inversely proportional to that product's demand elasticity. Charge the big mark-up where demand is inelastic, because those customers barely reduce consumption when the price rises, so you lose little surplus. Charge near marginal cost where demand is elastic, because there a mark-up would drive away lots of trade. Tune a single constant, the Ramsey number lambda, so that the mark-ups collectively just cover the fixed and common costs and the firm breaks even. Regulators nickname this "what the traffic will bear" or value-of-service pricing. Two refinements: name-check that in the single-product case you apply it across customer groups and it looks just like a monopolist price-discriminating, only at a lower overall level; and with substitutes or complements you replace simple elasticities with super-elasticities that fold in cross-price effects — smaller mark-ups for complements, larger for substitutes. The deepest point in the callout: the inverse-elasticity rule is the unifying thread of efficient pricing theory. Even optimal non-linear tariffs are just Ramsey pricing applied to successive increments of a single product.

The tariff

T = A + p·Q

4.2.5 · NON-LINEAR PRICING

Split the bill: a fixed fee plus marginal cost.

A two-part tariff T = A + p·Q charges an access fee A once, then p per unit. Under identical consumers the ideal is elegant: set A* = fixed cost per customer and p* = c = MC. You get first-best consumption and full cost recovery — no subsidy.

"Have your cake and eat it." The per-unit charge delivers allocative efficiency; the access charge covers F. Average outlay (A+p·Q)/Q falls as you consume more.
The two-part tariff is the first rule that lets us reach first-best without a subsidy, and it's the everyday shape of a utility bill — a standing charge plus a per-unit rate. Write the tariff as T equals A plus p times Q: a fixed access charge A levied once, and a variable charge p on every unit. Now here's the trick. The regulator faces two constraints — the firm must break even, and the access charge mustn't be so high that consumers refuse to participate. Under the strong assumption that all consumers are identical, the optimal solution is genuinely lovely: set the access charge equal to the fixed cost per customer, and set the per-unit charge equal to marginal cost. The per-unit price at marginal cost gives you allocative efficiency — first-best consumption, the full Q-prime output — while the fixed access charge quietly recovers F. Berg and Tschirhart call it having your cake and eating it too. Look at the figure. Marginal outlay — what you pay for the next unit — is flat at p, equal to marginal cost. But average outlay, total bill divided by quantity, starts high and falls toward p as you consume more, because the fixed A is being spread over more units — the shrinking gap A over Q. That declining average outlay is the signature of non-linear pricing, and it's the whole reason low-usage and high-usage customers experience the tariff so differently, which is exactly the problem the next slide exposes.

heterogeneity · self-selection · Pareto improvement (Willig)

4.2.6 · MENUS & OPTIMAL NON-LINEAR PRICING

Real consumers differ — so offer a menu.

The single two-part tariff assumes identical consumers. Drop that and a high fixed fee excludes low-usage customers who'd happily pay MC. Fix: offer a menu — a low-A/high-p tariff for light users, a high-A/low-p tariff for heavy users.

Self-selection. Each type picks the tariff that minimises its own bill (the dark line). Below Q* pick tariff 1; above Q* pick tariff 2. Willig (1978): making it optional can make everyone better off — a Pareto improvement.
Now we break the unrealistic assumption. Consumers are not identical — infrequent users value the service far less than intensive users, largely for income reasons. And that wrecks the single two-part tariff, because the fixed access charge that was fine for the average customer is a deal-breaker for a light user, who then drops out of the market entirely even though they'd gladly have paid marginal cost for a few units. That's a real efficiency loss and, worse, some consumers can be made worse off by the shift to two-part pricing. The fix is a menu. Offer two tariffs: tariff one has a low access charge but a high per-unit rate, designed to attract light users; tariff two has a high access charge but a low per-unit rate, attractive to heavy users. On the diagram each tariff is a line — its intercept is the access charge, its slope the per-unit price. A rational consumer picks whichever tariff minimises their total bill given expected consumption, so below the crossing point Q-star they take tariff one and above it tariff two. The bold lower envelope is what people actually choose. For this to work the light-user tariff's per-unit price must be high enough that heavy users don't defect to grab its low access charge — that's the self-selection constraint, and it's our first glimpse of incentive-compatibility, which dominates the rest of the chapter. Willig's striking result: make the tariff optional and you can make everyone better off — a genuine Pareto improvement over linear average-cost pricing.

Key terms

joint vs common costs · FDC · EPMU

4.3 & 4.3.1 · THE MULTI-PRODUCT FIRM

Real networks make many products — from shared costs.

A telecoms loop supplies local calls, long-distance, broadband, TV — from the same assets. That creates common costs: shared, not attributable to any one product. Price each product at MC and the deficit equals exactly the common cost F.

Joint vs common

Joint: fixed-proportion co-production. Common: needed for several products but not assignable to one. Both must be recovered somehow.

FDC method

Fully Distributed Costs — allocate F by each product's share of attributable cost, revenue or output. Long US history (ICC, FCC).

EPMU method

Equi-Proportionate Mark-Up — split F in proportion to each product's marginal cost. Simple, transparent.

The regulator's dirty secret. Cost-allocation methods are "simple, practical, gaming-resistant" — but arbitrary and economically inefficient. Prices end up bearing little relation to real costs. Crandall–Waverman put the US telecoms welfare loss at ~$8bn vs Ramsey.
Now we make the setting realistic in a second way: firms produce many products, not one. A gas or electricity utility differentiates by time of day, season and customer type; a telecoms network uses the very same exchanges, switches and copper to sell local calls, long-distance, mobile termination, broadband and cable TV. Marginal-cost pricing is still the efficient ideal for each product, but the moment products share assets you get common costs — costs necessary for several services but not attributable to any single one — and if you price everything at marginal cost the revenue shortfall equals exactly those common costs. Distinguish two flavours: joint costs, where products are co-produced in fixed proportions, and common costs, shared but not jointly produced. However you classify them, they must be recovered. In practice regulators reach for cost-allocation formulas. Fully Distributed Costs, FDC, shares the common cost out by each product's proportion of attributable cost, revenue or output — a method with a long American pedigree at the ICC and FCC. Equi-Proportionate Mark-Up, EPMU, allocates in proportion to each product's marginal cost. The worked example in the book: ten dollars of common cost, marginal costs of seventy and thirty, gives a uniform ten per cent mark-up. Regulators love these methods because they're simple, transparent and hard to game from accounting data. But — and Brown and Sibley call cost allocation the source of the most muddled proceedings in regulatory history — they are economically arbitrary. They recover the money but they don't maximise welfare, and Crandall and Waverman estimated the US telecoms welfare loss from using them instead of Ramsey pricing at around eight billion dollars.

Key terms

firm-peak vs shifting-peak · capacity cost causation

4.3.3 · PEAK-LOAD PRICING

Whoever causes the capacity, pays for it.

Demand swings by hour and season, but you can't store electricity, water pressure or bandwidth — so capacity is built for the peak. Peak-load pricing charges peak users the marginal operating + capital cost; off-peak users pay only marginal operating cost.

Firm-peak case. All the extra-capacity cost is recovered from peak prices alone. Off-peak prices fall near zero (operating costs are tiny). Treats peak and off-peak as separate products.
Peak-load pricing is a special, time-based application of the same "differentiate by willingness to pay" idea, and it's been used in electricity for decades. The physical fact driving it: you can't inventory these products. You can't store today's spare electricity, water pressure or midnight bandwidth for tomorrow's rush hour, so the network's physical capacity has to be big enough for the peak — a hot summer afternoon, the evening commute. That's expensive, and the question is who pays for the capacity. Peak-load pricing answers: the peak users, because they're the ones causing the capacity to be built. Peak prices are set to recover both the marginal operating cost and the marginal capital cost of a unit of peak capacity; off-peak prices recover only the marginal operating cost, because off-peak demand fits inside existing capacity. On the diagram the peak demand curve rams into the vertical capacity wall K-bar and clears at the high price P-peak, which includes the capacity cost; the off-peak demand curve clears down at operating cost, P-off, which in networks with tiny running costs is close to zero. In this standard firm-peak case, all the extra-capacity cost is borne by peak users alone. The elegance is that each class pays a price reflecting its own marginal cost, so you get allocative efficiency and cost recovery together — treating peak and off-peak consumption as genuinely separate products. Caveats: it assumes the periods are cleanly separable and demands independent, but if off-peak prices go too low people shift consumption — cheap overnight electricity led households to install storage heaters, blurring the peak.

4.3.3 · IN PRACTICE

Peak-load pricing needed a technology to arrive.

From tariffs to smart meters & real-time pricing

Efficient time-of-use pricing has always needed one thing regulators lacked: the ability to measure who consumes what, when. In water and energy that was historically hard. Recent smart-meter and smart-grid programmes change the game — jurisdictions now incentivise deployment precisely so marginal costs, which in electricity vary continuously with demand, can finally be reflected in real-time prices. The intellectual roots run to William Vickrey, who proposed congestion pricing for the New York subway in 1952 and later for roads. Peak-load theory sat on the shelf for decades not because it was wrong, but because the metering to implement it didn't exist.

Think about it. GB's smart-meter rollout finally makes half-hourly settlement and dynamic tariffs feasible for households. Which of this chapter's pricing rules does that unlock — and which consumer group might lose out if inelastic, low-income users can't shift load?
This case study makes a point that matters far beyond the textbook: the gap between pricing theory and pricing practice is often a measurement gap, not a theory gap. Peak-load and real-time pricing have been well understood since the 1950s, but implementing them requires knowing exactly who consumed how much at each moment, and for most of the twentieth century that information simply wasn't available in water and energy — meters were read quarterly, if that. What's changed is metering technology. Smart meters and smart-grid investments, which many regulators including Ofgem now actively incentivise, generate the granular, real-time usage data that dynamic pricing has always needed. Electricity is the poster child because its marginal cost genuinely varies minute to minute with demand, so real-time prices can track real-time costs. Vickrey is the intellectual grandfather here — congestion pricing for the New York subway in 1952, later extended to roads, the same idea of charging users the marginal cost they impose at the moment they impose it. The exercise asks students to connect this to Britain's own rollout and, importantly, to the distributional catch: dynamic pricing rewards those who can shift load, and may penalise inelastic, low-income households who can't — a fairness concern that, as we'll see at the end, is exactly why regulators resist efficient-but-inequitable pricing.

Key terms

principal–agent · information rent · hidden information

4.4 & 4.4.1 · ASYMMETRIC INFORMATION

The firm knows its costs. You don't.

Drop full information — the realistic case. The firm knows more about its costs, technology and effort, creating a principal–agent problem: it can overstate costs to win higher prices. Loeb–Magat (1979): still reach first-best by letting the firm pick its own price and subsidising it the entire consumer surplus at that price.

Why it works. Getting the whole surplus, the firm maximises it — which means pricing at MC (P*). Any other price shrinks its own payoff. Cost cuts flow straight to profit, so it minimises cost too. Snag: consumers get nothing.
We now pull away the biggest fiction of all: that the regulator knows the firm's costs. In reality the firm always knows more — about its costs, its production technology, and crucially how much effort it's putting into cost reduction. That's the principal-agent problem at the heart of modern regulation: the regulator is the principal trying to control an agent who holds superior information and can exploit it, classically by overstating costs to justify higher prices. The information rent is the profit the firm earns purely from that private knowledge, and the central trade-off of the whole literature is between minimising that rent and preserving incentives for efficiency. Loeb and Magat's 1979 model is the surprising opening move: even under hidden cost information you can still hit first-best. Let the firm choose its own price, and have the regulator pay it a subsidy equal to the entire consumer surplus at whatever price it picks. Watch the figure. If the firm captures all the consumer surplus, then maximising its own profit is identical to maximising total surplus — and total surplus is maximised at price equals marginal cost, output Q-star. Charge any higher price and it forfeits the little deadweight triangle, cutting its own payoff, so it voluntarily prices at MC. And because every pound of cost it saves drops straight to its bottom line, it minimises cost too. Allocative and productive efficiency both achieved, and the regulator never has to verify a cost figure. The catch is glaring and fatal for real use: producers scoop the entire surplus and consumers get nothing. It solves efficiency by ignoring distribution entirely.

4.4.1–4.4.3 · WHEN DISTRIBUTION MATTERS

Once the regulator cares about rent, first-best dies.

Loeb–Magat only worked because the regulator didn't mind the firm keeping everything. Put weight on consumer surplus (α < 1) and the models bite. The recurring result: to limit the firm's rent, you accept some inefficiency.

The menu insight (Laffont–Tirole). Offer contracts with different cost-sharing: a low-cost firm picks the high-powered fixed-price contract (keeps savings, bears risk); a high-cost firm picks cost-plus (safe, low transfer). The firm reveals its type by choosing.
Loeb–Magat was a special case that only worked because the regulator was indifferent to who got the surplus. The moment the regulator puts more weight on consumers than on the firm's profit — alpha below one — the clean result collapses and we enter the genuine world of regulatory economics, where every model delivers the same hard message: to claw back the firm's information rent, you must tolerate some inefficiency. Walk the table. Baron and Myerson, 1982: the firm hides its costs, an adverse-selection problem, and the optimal answer is to set price above marginal cost — accepting allocative inefficiency — because that's what limits the rent you have to pay a low-cost firm to reveal itself. Laffont and Tirole, 1986: now the firm hides its effort, a moral-hazard problem, and the regulator observes cost but can't tell whether high cost means a genuinely high-cost firm or a lazy one. The elegant solution is a menu of contracts with different degrees of cost-sharing — that's the callout. An efficient firm self-selects the high-powered fixed-price contract, keeping the savings it generates but bearing the risk; an inefficient firm picks the cost-plus contract, safe but with a low transfer. Here prices track marginal cost but costs themselves sit above the optimum — the deviation moves from price to cost. Finally Riordan and Lewis–Sappington handle hidden demand information, where the optimal policy flips depending on whether marginal costs rise or fall with output. The unifying lesson Armstrong and Sappington draw: optimal regulated prices can end up above, below, or at marginal cost depending entirely on what the firm knows — there is no single right answer, only the trade-off.

Key terms

SRMC vs LRMC · commitment · ratchet effect · regulatory lag

4.5 · PRICING OVER TIME

Add time, and expectations change everything.

SRMC vs LRMC (4.5.1)

SRMC is efficient each period but volatile, and discourages capacity investment when congestion rents are high. LRMC de-links price from short-run cost → steadier signals, better dynamic efficiency. UK energy, telecoms & water lean on LRMC.

Incremental Surplus Subsidy (4.5.2)

Sappington–Sibley: pay the firm the change in consumer surplus, minus last period's profit. Firm rents evaporate after period 1; prices converge to MC.

Vogelsang–Finsinger (4.5.3)

No transfers needed. Constrain next period's prices so, applied to this period's output, they can't exceed observed cost. Prices ratchet toward Ramsey–Boiteux.

Yardstick competition (4.5.4)

Shleifer (1985): tie each firm's allowed price to comparable firms' costs. Regional water/energy DNOs "compete" on paper. Fails if firms aren't truly alike.

The commitment problem & the ratchet. If the regulator can't commit to ignore period-1 information, the firm anticipates a future squeeze and overstates costs today — or mimics an efficient rival then "takes the money and runs" (Laffont–Tirole). Commitment is worth its weight in gold.
Reality intrudes a fourth time: regulation isn't a one-shot game, it runs for decades over repeated reviews, and that transforms the analysis because now expectations drive behaviour. Four ideas. First, the short-run versus long-run marginal cost debate. Short-run MC is the most efficient price in any single period, but in a multi-period world it's volatile and, worse, it discourages investment — when demand is high and capacity tight, SRMC prices spike, and adding capacity would kill those congestion rents, so the firm holds back. Long-run MC deliberately de-links price from short-run cost to give steadier signals and better dynamic efficiency, which is why UK energy, telecoms and water regulators generally price off long-run cost estimates. Second, the incremental surplus subsidy scheme of Sappington and Sibley — a clever fix to Loeb–Magat's giveaway: pay the firm only the change in consumer surplus each period minus last period's profit, and the firm earns rent only in period one, then prices at marginal cost forever after with surplus flowing back to consumers. Third, Vogelsang–Finsinger, which needs no transfers at all: constrain next period's prices so that, valued at this period's output, they can't exceed observed cost, and prices ratchet down toward Ramsey–Boiteux over time. Fourth, yardstick competition — Shleifer's idea of tying each regional monopoly's allowed price to the costs of comparable firms, so England's water companies effectively compete against each other on paper, though it breaks down if they're not genuinely similar. And overarching all of it, the commitment problem in the callout: if the firm believes the regulator will use today's revealed low costs to squeeze it tomorrow — the ratchet effect — it will inflate costs now, or mimic an efficient firm and then quit. The ability to commit not to exploit information is one of the most valuable things a regulator can have.

4.6 · BEYOND PRICE

Price isn't the only lever — quality and cost are too.

Quality is a choice (4.6.1)

Reliability, safety, responsiveness — all endogenous. An unregulated monopolist may supply too much or too little quality. Regulators use minimum standards and target-linked penalties/rewards.

The observability trap

If neither regulator nor consumer can observe quality until after the fact (call clarity, supply reliability), the firm's information advantage means optimal regulation often delegates discretion plus incentive payments.

Network-effect quality

When networks interconnect, a firm may under-provide quality because it can't capture the full benefit — the interconnecting network's quality matters too.

Cost efficiency is the real prize (4.6.2)

Costs are endogenous too. In practice regulators (and privatisation/restructuring) chase cost reduction far harder than allocative efficiency.

Price control shapes quality. When prices track costs (rate-of-return), firms will spend to raise quality. When prices are de-linked (price caps), quality incentives can weaken — a tension Chapter 5 tackles head-on.
The final section widens the lens beyond pricing, because a monopolist can exploit customers in two other ways the price rules ignore: by letting quality slide and by not bothering to cut costs. Start with quality. Everything so far assumed a fixed, uniform quality of service, but quality is endogenous — the firm chooses it, across dimensions like reliability, safety, responsiveness to complaints and new connections. The theory is genuinely ambiguous: an unregulated monopolist can supply either more or less quality than is socially optimal, depending on how marginal versus infra-marginal customers value it. So regulators intervene with minimum standards and, increasingly, service targets backed by financial penalties and rewards. The deep difficulty is observability — with things like call clarity or supply reliability, neither the regulator nor even the consumer knows the quality until after they've experienced it, so the firm's information advantage is huge, and optimal quality regulation often means delegating discretion to the firm while attaching incentive payments to measurable outcomes. There's also a network-effects wrinkle: when two networks interconnect, each may under-invest in quality because it can't capture the full benefit. Then cost efficiency, and here's the punchline of the whole chapter for a practitioner: although we've spent twenty slides on allocative efficiency and clever prices, in the real world regulators care far more about cost reduction. Privatisation and restructuring were sold overwhelmingly on the promise of lower costs, not textbook-optimal prices. And critically, the form of price control you choose shapes both quality and cost incentives — rate-of-return regulation reimburses cost so it encourages quality spending but dulls cost-cutting, while price caps do the reverse. That trade-off is exactly what Chapter 5, the next module, is about.

Under Ramsey–Boiteux pricing, which product carries the largest mark-up over marginal cost?

A
The product with the most elastic demand.

Backwards. Elastic customers flee a mark-up, so they're priced near marginal cost.

B
The product with the most inelastic demand.

✓ Correct. Mark-up ∝ 1/η. Inelastic demand barely responds to price, so loading the fixed-cost recovery there minimises the surplus lost — "what the traffic will bear".

C
The product with the highest marginal cost.

Irrelevant. Ramsey mark-ups key off elasticity, not the level of marginal cost.

A quick self-check on the single most important pricing rule in the chapter. The inverse-elasticity rule says the mark-up over marginal cost is proportional to one over the elasticity of demand. So the biggest mark-up lands on the most inelastic product — answer B. The intuition is the whole point of Ramsey pricing: if you have to raise prices above marginal cost somewhere to recover fixed costs, do it where consumers respond least, because that's where you destroy the least trade. Answer A gets it exactly backwards — elastic customers would abandon the product if you marked it up, so you price them near marginal cost. Answer C is a classic trap: the level of marginal cost is irrelevant to the size of the Ramsey mark-up; it's the elasticity that matters, not how expensive the product is to make. If a learner picks A, they've inverted the rule — send them back to slide 11. If they pick C, they've confused cost level with cost recovery. The equity sting worth mentioning: the most inelastic customers are often the captive, essential-need, sometimes lower-income users — which is precisely why regulators find Ramsey pricing efficient but politically unpalatable.

4.7 · Conclusion

efficiency vs equity · information limits · conceptual clarity

4.7 · THE PRACTITIONER'S PUZZLE

If Ramsey is so efficient, why won't regulators use it?

Ofgem, Ofcom, the CAA, the ICC and Electricité de France — even with Boiteux himself as chairman — have all resisted Ramsey–Boiteux pricing. The chapter closes by explaining the gap between theory and practice.

① Impracticable

It needs reliable demand-elasticity data the regulator rarely has. The ICC: "too difficult and burdensome for universal application."

② Equity, not just efficiency

Efficient ≠ fair. Loading mark-ups on inelastic (often captive, essential-need) customers raises distributive objections. Postcomm & Ofgem cite fairness.

③ Information asymmetry is severe

The optimal-pricing models assume knowledge regulators simply don't possess in practice.

④ Still influential

Even unused, the theory shaped incentive regulation — menu contracts, yardstick competition — and gives conceptual clarity on the trade-offs.

This is Decker's conclusion, and it's the most important practitioner takeaway in the module — the reason a working economist should care about all this theory even though almost none of it is applied literally. Ramsey–Boiteux pricing has, as Decker notes, endeared itself to economists for decades, yet regulator after regulator refuses to use it: Ofgem, Ofcom, the Civil Aviation Authority, the American ICC — and the killer anecdote, even Electricité de France declined to use it despite Marcel Boiteux, co-author of the theory, sitting as its chairman. Why? Four reasons. First, it's impracticable — it demands reliable demand-elasticity estimates that regulators just don't have, which is why the ICC called it too burdensome for universal use. Second, and this is the big one, efficiency is not equity: Ramsey pricing deliberately loads the heaviest mark-ups onto the most inelastic customers, who are frequently the captive, essential-need, sometimes poorest users, and that triggers fairness objections — Postcomm and Ofgem have explicitly rejected it partly on distributive grounds. Third, the information asymmetry in the real world is far more severe than the models assume. But — and here's the redemption — the fourth point is that these theories, even unimplemented, have profoundly shaped practice. Menu regulation, yardstick competition, incentive price caps all descend directly from this analytical work. As Decker puts it, the value of the theory isn't a cookbook of prices; it's conceptual clarity about the trade-offs, and that clarity is exactly what a good regulator carries into every price review.
