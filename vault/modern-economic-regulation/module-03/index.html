Do we even need
a regulator?

Traditional price-and-entry regulation is one option among several. This module walks the six real alternatives — competition for the market, contestability, state ownership, ex-post competition law, deregulation, and negotiated settlements — and asks, for each, when it is genuinely viable.

Welcome to Module 3. Chapter 2 gave us the rationales for regulating utilities — natural monopoly, market power, externalities. This chapter turns the question around and asks the awkward follow-up: granted there's a problem, is a dedicated ex-ante regulator really the best tool for fixing it? Decker lays out six alternatives that have been proposed or actually used to control utility behaviour without conventional regulation. Some are live in practice today — franchising, state ownership, deregulation. Others are more influential as ideas that shaped how we regulate — contestability theory especially. Our through-line across these twenty-one slides is simple: each alternative works only under specific conditions, and the practitioner's job is to know which conditions hold in the industry in front of them. Four objectives frame the module. Notice the logic running through them. Objective one is a posture — Kahn's "is this really necessary?" applied to the regulator's own existence. The six alternatives

franchising · contestability · state ownership · competition law · deregulation · negotiated settlement

3.1 · IS ECONOMIC REGULATION INEVITABLE?

Six ways to control a utility — without a price cap.

Chapter 2 said natural monopoly and market power justify intervention. It didn't say that intervention has to be a standing, sector-specific regulator writing prices in advance. Decker lines up six substitutes and holds each to one test: are its costs and benefits better than standard regulation's?

The recurring caveat. The answer is almost always "it depends on the activity." Core network layers (wires, pipes) behave differently from the competitive layers around them — so the right answer often differs within a single industry.
This decision tree is my construction, not Decker's — but it captures the spirit of section 3.1 exactly. Start at the top: we've established there's a market-failure problem worth addressing. Now walk down. Could firms compete to win the whole market at the outset? If yes, franchise bidding might substitute for regulation — that's Demsetz, next few slides. If not, are entry and exit costless, so the mere threat of a hit-and-run entrant disciplines the incumbent? If yes, the market is contestable and you can leave it alone — that's Baumol. If not, might general competition law, applied after the fact, be sufficient? If yes, no dedicated regulator needed. Only when all those doors close do you land on traditional ex-ante regulation, or its cousin, state ownership. The crucial practical point is in the callout: you don't answer this once per industry, you answer it activity by activity — the pipes may need regulating while retail supply over them does not.

Key terms

franchise bidding · Demsetz (1968) · Chadwick (1859) · competition for the field

3.2 · COMPETITION FOR THE MARKET

Auction the monopoly instead of regulating it.

Demsetz's 1968 challenge: scale economies in production say nothing about the number of firms that can bid to supply. Let potential providers compete at the tender stage — lowest price wins the exclusive franchise — and the competitive price falls out of the auction. The rivalry moves from inside the market to the contest for it.

The promised benefits. Cuts the regulator's information burden (no need to estimate costs and demand), sidesteps rate-of-return over-investment, and gives the winner a direct incentive to cut costs — it keeps the savings while it runs the contract.
Harold Demsetz's 1968 paper is one of the great "wait, why do we assume that?" moments in regulatory economics — building on Edwin Chadwick's nineteenth-century idea of competition for the field rather than within it. His move is to sever two things everyone had bundled together: yes, a natural-monopoly industry has scale economies so only one firm should ultimately supply it — but that says nothing about how many firms can compete to be that one firm at the tender stage. If enough of them bid, and they can't collude, the winning bid gets driven down to average cost, the firm earns a normal profit, and you've achieved the competitive outcome with no ongoing price regulation at all. The regulator's role shrinks to running a clean auction. The benefits listed in the callout are real and attractive: it dodges the regulator's crippling information problem and the notorious over-investment incentive of rate-of-return regulation. Note the conditions bottom-left of the figure — those are exactly where the next slide attacks.

Key terms

incomplete contracts · transaction-cost economics · Williamson · bidder parity

3.2 · WHY THE AUCTION ISN'T THE HARD PART

The contract, not the tender, is where it fails.

Competition for the market is essentially a long-term contract between the winner and the government. And, as Williamson and the transaction-cost school stressed, complex utility contracts cannot be fully specified up front. Once you allow for real institutions, the "complications" of regulation come back through the side door.

Incompleteness

Long-duration, high-uncertainty contracts can't foresee everything. Gaps become a source of instability and renegotiation.

Adaptation & hazards

Adjusting prices to cost changes, enforcing quality, obscure award criteria, ex-post opportunism — all costly to manage.

Bidder parity at renewal

The incumbent knows the asset and the customers. Re-tendering is rarely a level playing field, so rivalry decays over time.

The verdict of Armstrong, Cowan & Vickers (1994). Bidding is not well suited to capital-intensive utility activities, and administering the contract needs ongoing regulatory involvement anyway. The difference between "franchise administration" and "regulation" becomes one of degree, not kind.
Here's the counter-punch, and it's decisive for capital-heavy networks. The elegant auction is the easy 10% of the problem. The hard 90% is the contract the winner then operates under, often for decades. Oliver Williamson's transaction-cost work — famously using cable TV franchises — showed you simply cannot write a complete contract for a complex, long-lived, uncertain utility service. So you get contractual incompleteness, and then all the mess: how do you adjust the price when costs move, how do you police quality, how do you stop the franchisor behaving opportunistically once it's locked in? And the killer for repeated use is bidder parity — at renewal the incumbent holds the assets, the operational knowledge and the customer relationships, so the "competition" at the second tender is a shadow of the first. Armstrong, Cowan and Vickers concluded bidding just isn't right for capital-intensive utility activities, and that once you're administering the contract you're doing something that looks an awful lot like regulation. Degree, not kind. That said — Zupan and Prager found franchising works better empirically than the theory's critics feared, so it's a genuine debate, not a settled dismissal.

Where it lives

gas · Paris 1800s · US municipal utilities · rail & water concessions · developing economies

3.2 · IN THE WILD

A century of franchising — with mixed results.

Concessions and franchises, 1859 → today

Nineteenth-century Paris put gas supply up for "competition for the whole field," and US cities routinely franchised utility services. Concession contracts remain a live feature today — rail and water concessions in many countries, and especially in transitional and developing economies, where courts are sometimes seen as less politically capturable than regulatory agencies (Gómez-Ibáñez). London Underground even franchised electricity distribution — an outcome Littlechild judged the opposite of Williamson's cautionary cable-TV tale.

What tends to go wrong

Empirical studies (Guasch, Marques & Berg, Estache) find the predicted problems recur: poor contract design, weak bidding, and renegotiation — plus the bidder-parity problem at re-franchising.

What makes it work

Recent US concessions perform better — because they're used in simpler settings (not electricity or telecoms) and for shorter durations. Both favour the contractual approach.

Bottom line. Franchise bidding is, at best, a short-to-medium-run answer to natural monopoly — perhaps most useful early in a new industry's life. Over a long horizon, the classic problems of regulation tend to re-emerge.
Don't let the theory critique fool you into thinking franchising is dead — it's everywhere. Paris gas in the 1800s, American municipal utility franchises, and today a large slice of the world's rail and water infrastructure runs on concession contracts, particularly in the developing world. Gómez-Ibáñez's nice observation: developing countries sometimes prefer concessions precisely because they trust the courts to enforce a contract more than they trust a regulatory agency to resist political pressure. The empirical record is genuinely mixed. The transaction-cost problems the last slide flagged do recur — Guasch's work on Latin American concessions found renegotiation was rife. But — and this is the practical lesson — the arrangements that work share two features: they're used in simpler activities, not the fiendishly complex ones like electricity, and they run for shorter periods, which limits how much uncertainty the contract has to absorb. So the honest verdict is Viscusi's: franchising is a decent short-to-medium-run fix, maybe best at the birth of a new natural monopoly, but not a durable long-run substitute for regulation.

Key terms

contestable market · hit-and-run entry · shadow entrants · Baumol (1982)

3.3 · CONTESTABILITY & THE THREAT OF ENTRY

Discipline by the entrant who never has to arrive.

Baumol's early-1980s theory: where "entry is absolutely free and exit is absolutely costless," a market is perfectly contestable. Any excess profit is an instant earnings opportunity — an entrant swoops in, undercuts, takes the profit, and exits costlessly. Knowing this, even a single incumbent prices at average cost. The threat alone does the work.

Shadow entrants. Potential competition becomes almost as powerful as actual competition. On this logic, banning entry can be harmful — it removes the very shadow entrants that keep the incumbent honest.
Contestability theory caused a genuine stir when Baumol, Panzar and Willig developed it in the early 1980s. The claim is radical: you might not need regulation, or even actual competition, to get competitive behaviour — you just need the credible threat of it. If entry is absolutely free and exit absolutely costless, then the moment the incumbent lifts price above average cost to grab a profit, a hit-and-run entrant appears, undercuts, banks the profit and vanishes — no harm done to itself because exit costs nothing. My figure shows the price trying to spike above average cost and being instantly slapped back down. A rational incumbent, anticipating this, never lets the profit opportunity open in the first place. Baumol's phrase: "every deviation from good behaviour instantly makes them vulnerable to hit-and-run entry." Those disciplining phantoms are the shadow entrants, and the striking policy corollary is that entry restrictions become actively counterproductive — they remove the very threat that was keeping prices down. Extraordinary if true. The next slide asks whether it is.

Key terms

sunk cost · strategic entry deterrence · normative benchmark · Stiglitz critique

3.3 · THE FINE PRINT

Three heroic assumptions — and a fatal sensitivity.

① No sunk costs

All entry costs recoverable on exit. This is the crux, and what separates contestability from franchise bidding.

② Identical technology

Entrants can serve the same demand with the same costs, information and products as the incumbent — "total entry".

③ Incumbent can't react in time

Entry and exit happen before the incumbent adjusts price. No strategic entry deterrence.

Stiglitz's demolition. "The presence of arbitrarily small sunk costs can serve as an absolute barrier to entry and make potential competition completely ineffective." Add a tiny entry lag, and the whole result unravels. Critics call policy lessons "naive and premature."

The proponents' comeback

Perfect contestability was never meant to describe the world — it's a normative benchmark, like perfect competition, useful for delineating where regulation is and isn't needed. Baumol & Willig: the theory's real contribution was as a guide to regulation, not an argument for abolishing it.

Three assumptions carry the whole edifice, and all three are demanding. First and most important: no sunk costs — every penny of entry cost recoverable on the way out. That's what makes exit costless, and it's precisely what real network industries violate. Second, total entry — the entrant can instantly replicate the incumbent in scale, technology, brand, everything. Third, the incumbent is a sitting duck — it can't cut its own price before the entrant has come and gone. Stiglitz landed the decisive blow: even arbitrarily small sunk costs can act as an absolute barrier to entry, and even a short entry lag lets the incumbent respond, and either one destroys the result. Shepherd called the policy claims naive and premature. Now, the proponents don't concede the field — and this is the sophisticated bit — they say you've misread the theory. Perfect contestability is a benchmark, exactly like perfect competition, which also doesn't literally exist. Its value is as a yardstick for asking "how close is this market, and therefore how much regulation does it actually need?" On that reading contestability's legacy is as a tool that shaped regulation, not one that abolished it — which is where it genuinely landed.

Key terms

capital on wheels · asset specificity · stand-alone cost (SAC) · incremental cost

3.3 · CAPITAL ON WHEELS vs CAPITAL IN THE GROUND

Contestable where the assets can move on.

Mobile capital → more contestable

Airlines, trucking, buses — "capital on wheels." Planes and lorries redeploy to other routes, so capital is fixed but not sunk. Yet even here, US airline deregulation gave contestability little empirical support — entry proved far from "ultrafree."

Sunk capital → weak threat

Electricity wires, gas pipes, water mains, telephone lines — durable, immobile, specific. Large sunk costs mean rapid entry and exit are impossible, so the entry threat on core networks is weak. Regulation stays.

But not the whole industry. Unbundle the supply chain and the picture splits. Generation, gas production and retail supply — the layers that use the network — may be contestable even where the network owner is not. That insight underpins the whole deregulation agenda (section 3.6).

The lasting legacy

Contestability's concepts of stand-alone cost and incremental cost became the accepted basis for setting price ceilings and floors in railways and telecoms — the theory's most durable practical contribution.

So where does contestability actually bite? The test is asset specificity — Williamson's phrase, and he noted contestability and transaction-cost economics are looking at the same phenomenon through opposite ends of the telescope. Where capital is mobile — airlines, trucking, buses — you get "capital on wheels": the plane is expensive but you can fly it to a different route tomorrow, so the cost is fixed but not sunk. That's the best real-world candidate for contestability. And yet — this is a lovely humbling fact — when the US actually deregulated airlines, the theory's predictions largely failed. Entry on specific routes was difficult, nothing like ultrafree. At the other extreme sit the core utility networks: wires, pipes, mains, lines. Durable, immobile, hugely sunk. The entry threat there is essentially nil, so regulation stays put. But — the callout — once you unbundle the supply chain, the layers sitting on top of the network, like generation and retail, can be contestable even when the wires beneath them aren't. Hold that thought; it's the seed of section 3.6. And note the legacy: stand-alone and incremental cost, born in this theory, now set the price ceilings and floors regulators actually use in rail and telecoms.

Key terms

principal–agent · public-interest objective · shareholder executive · Vickers & Yarrow

3.4 · STATE OWNERSHIP OF THE UTILITIES

Own it, and you needn't price-cap it — in theory.

The economic logic: a state-owned firm isn't driven by private gain, so it can be directed to act well — price at cost, invest efficiently. Historically the default control method across much of the world, before the 1980s–90s privatisation wave (Chile, UK, Argentina, Australia).

The principal–agent snag. Swap shareholders for "the state" and the principal's objective becomes fuzzy — welfare, employment, low prices, regional politics, all at once, and shifting every election. Managers serve a principal that can't say clearly what it wants.
State ownership is alternative number three, and for most of the twentieth century it was the default across large parts of the world. The economic argument is disarmingly simple: a state firm isn't chasing private profit, so the government can just instruct it to behave — price at cost, invest for the long term, serve everyone. No price cap needed because you own the thing. The trouble shows up the moment you apply the principal–agent lens, which my figure lays out side by side. Under private ownership the principal is shareholders with one crisp objective — profit — and a strong pecuniary incentive to monitor managers closely. Under state ownership the "principal" is a committee of the whole nation — politicians, civil servants, the public — wanting welfare and employment and low prices and rural votes, objectives that conflict and that change with every administration. The manager is being asked to optimise something nobody can define, monitored by officials whose own incentives to watch closely are weak. Herbert Morrison hoped public boards would be "high custodians of the public interest"; the incentive structure doesn't reliably produce that. Hence tools like shareholder executives to sharpen monitoring — and, as we'll see, independent regulators bolted on top.

3.4 · WHAT THE DATA SAY — AND THE HYBRID

Privatisation usually helps; state ownership rarely stands alone.

Evidence approachFinding
Cross-country & within-country comparisonsBroadly favour privately owned, regulated firms — though early studies biased by measuring only profitability/cost, not welfare.
Privatisation event studies (1980s–90s)Performance gains "almost unanimously" reported across the economy; supported in telecoms, rail, energy.
Developing-country experienceMore mixed — private ownership often did not improve infrastructure performance (Estache & Wren-Lewis).
The telling modern pattern. State-owned utilities are increasingly subjected to the same independent price regulation as private ones — UK rail, Scottish Water, Australian/NZ/Irish networks. Ownership and regulation coexist rather than substitute — which suggests state ownership on its own isn't seen as an effective control.
Think about it. If a state-owned firm answers to both a government owner and an independent regulator with conflicting aims — network expansion vs cost scrutiny — the firm "works for two masters." What separation of roles would you design to stop the signals colliding?
Two empirical questions occupied economists here: do the incentive stories actually show up in the data, and what happens when ownership changes? The privatisation event studies from the 80s and 90s are the strongest evidence — Megginson and Netter's survey found performance improvements almost across the board, echoed in telecoms, rail and energy. But read the caveats. Early comparisons were biased toward private firms because they measured profitability and cost, not consumer welfare — a state firm deliberately pricing below cost looks "inefficient" on those metrics when it's actually pursuing a distributional goal. And in developing countries the private-is-better result frequently didn't hold. Now the punchline in the callout, which is what a practitioner should really take away: look at what governments actually do now. They increasingly regulate their own state-owned utilities with an independent agency — UK rail, Scottish Water, networks in Australia, New Zealand and Ireland. If state ownership were a genuine substitute for regulation you wouldn't need to do that. The fact that they layer regulation on top is a quiet admission that ownership alone doesn't discipline behaviour well enough. And that creates the "two masters" problem in the exercise — owner and regulator pulling different ways — which is a real live design challenge.

Key terms

ex ante · ex post · rules vs standards · regulatory error

3.5 · RELIANCE ON EX-POST COMPETITION LAW

Write the rules first, or sue afterwards?

Instead of a regulator setting prices in advance, leave firms unconstrained but threaten ex-post intervention if conduct proves harmful. This is the choice between a rules-based and a standards-based regime.

 Ex ante — regulationEx post — competition law
TimingLimits conduct before it occursIntervenes only after harm appears
InformationMust guess costs/demand up front → regulatory errorBetter information — the effects are already visible
CertaintyHigh: allowable conduct is delineatedLower: an open standard can be breached many ways
Best whereHarm frequent, large, irreversibleHarm occasional; targeted intervention suffices
The attraction. No permanent sector regulator, no ex-ante price rules, intervention only when problems arise, and less "regulatory game-playing." You just need a competent, well-resourced competition authority.
Section 3.5 is really about a deep distinction from law-and-economics: do you control conduct ex ante or ex post? Ex ante is the regulator's way — anticipate the bad behaviour and rule it out in advance with a price cap or a licence condition. Ex post is the competition-law way — let the firm act freely, then intervene after the fact if the conduct turns out to be harmful. The table lays out the trade-off cleanly. Ex ante gives certainty — everyone knows the allowable prices — but at the cost of regulatory error, because you're forecasting costs and demand you can't really know, and that's brutal in fast-moving industries. Ex post has the opposite profile: much better information, because by the time you act the effects are visible, but much less certainty, because it's a standards-based regime and a general standard can be violated in ways nobody specified in advance. The decision rule at the bottom is the useful one: ex ante is right where harm would be frequent, large and irreversible; ex post is right where harm is occasional and targeted intervention is enough. And the appeal of going ex-post is institutional minimalism — no standing regulator at all, just a good competition authority. Next slide: what that authority actually does to a utility.

Key terms

abuse of dominance · essential facility · excessive pricing · refusal to supply

3.5 · TWO KINDS OF REDRESS — AND A CAUTIONARY TALE

Antitrust can reach a utility two ways.

Exclusionary conduct

Refusing rivals access to a bottleneck / essential facility on reasonable terms — e.g. the local loop in telephony, or electricity transmission and distribution networks in Europe.

Exploitative conduct

Charging excessive prices — actionable in Europe, but deeply controversial: when is a price "too high"? Price–cost tests are treacherous in multi-product settings.

New Zealand's "light-handed" experiment, 1990s

NZ tried to run its utilities on economy-wide competition law plus a few light-handed measures — no dedicated ex-ante regulator. It is generally judged to have failed, and was replaced by a more standard ex-ante regulatory approach. In the UK and Australia, where regulators hold both competition-law and ex-ante powers, there's been a clear tendency to reach for the ex-ante tools.

Complement, not substitute. In European telecoms, ex-ante access regulation and abuse-of-dominance provisions apply concurrently. Contrast the US: antitrust generally won't apply where a regulator already has power to enforce access (Trinko, 2004).
If you go the competition-law route, the law gives you two handles on a utility. The exclusionary one is refusal to supply — a dominant firm denying rivals access to an essential or bottleneck facility they need to compete. The essential-facilities doctrine goes back to the 1912 Terminal Railroad case in the US; in Europe the local loop and the electricity networks are the classic candidates. The exploitative handle is excessive pricing — charging too much — which Europe will act on but which is genuinely hard, because deciding when a price is "excessive" means price-cost comparisons that get very messy once a firm sells multiple products and you have to allocate fixed costs. Now the case study, which is the memorable bit. New Zealand in the 1990s actually ran the experiment — tried to govern its utilities with general competition law plus a light touch, no dedicated regulator. It's widely regarded as a failure and they reverted to ex-ante regulation. And where regulators hold both sets of powers, as in the UK and Australia, they reach for the ex-ante tools in practice. The nuance to carry forward: it's often complement not substitute — European telecoms runs both concurrently — whereas the US Trinko decision walls antitrust off where a regulator already governs access.

Key terms

3.6 · 'DEREGULATION' & RELIANCE ON COMPETITION

Change the structure, then let competition do the work.

The fifth alternative applies only where an industry is already regulated: withdraw ex-ante restrictions on entry and price for the activities that can bear competition, and rely on competition itself to discipline conduct. Decker prefers "restructuring" — because it usually means re-regulation, new rules for the new structure, not an absence of rules.

Why competition, when it works, wins. Hayek's "discovery procedure" — competition generates facts, cost-cutting and innovation that no regulator could specify ex ante — and it dissolves the regulator's information-asymmetry problem entirely.
This is the alternative most working regulators actually spend their careers on. It only arises in an already-regulated industry, and the move is structural: instead of regulating a vertically integrated monopoly end to end, you break the chain into layers, ring-fence the genuinely monopolistic core network, and open the layers around it — generation, retail — to competition. My figure shows the before-and-after: one integrated block becomes a regulated network sandwiched between competitive generation and competitive retail. Decker is careful about the word. He prefers "restructuring" to "deregulation," and quotes Vogel's line that deregulation usually turns into re-regulation — you're not removing rules, you're writing new ones tailored to the new structure, licences and access codes and switching protocols. Why bother? Because competition, where it genuinely works, beats regulation on its own terms — Hayek's discovery procedure, competition as a process that turns up new information, lower costs and innovations no regulator could have written into a price control. And crucially it dissolves the information asymmetry that hobbles every regulator. The catch, next slide, is that opening a market dominated by a legacy incumbent is far harder than it sounds.

Key terms

asymmetric regulation · incumbency advantage · customer inertia · switching costs

3.6.1 · HELPING COMPETITION ALONG

Level the pitch — or tilt it toward the entrant?

A former monopolist enters the new competitive era with enormous legacy advantages: long-term input contracts on favourable terms, a household brand (British Telecom, France Télécom, EDF), and customers who won't switch. Regulators respond with asymmetric regulation — different rules for different firms.

Forms it takes

Restrict the incumbent's ability to cut prices in response to entry; let entrants charge asymmetric mark-ups; lighter reporting for new firms; supplier-of-last-resort duties on the incumbent; measures to cut search & switching costs (number portability, comparison sites).

The objections

Distorts price signals and biases toward particular firms/technologies; creates competitive distortions; and dampens incumbent investment while making entrant investment excessive — a "general consensus" that it induces inefficient entry.

An "infant industry" argument in disguise. Schmalensee coined the term for the FCC's tougher rules on AT&T than its rivals. Supporters see a powerful pro-consumer lever; sceptics (Armstrong & Sappington) warn the costs often outweigh the benefits.
Opening a market isn't as simple as repealing the entry ban, because the former monopolist doesn't start level — it starts miles ahead. It has the long-term supply contracts locked up on good terms, it has a brand that's often literally the name of the country plus the service, and it has customers who exhibit remarkable inertia and face real switching costs. So regulators reach for asymmetric regulation: deliberately different, usually tougher, rules for the incumbent than for entrants. The toolkit is on the left — you might stop the incumbent from dropping prices to crush entry, let entrants charge higher mark-ups to make entry attractive, impose supplier-of-last-resort duties on the incumbent, and attack switching costs directly with things like number portability. But — and Decker gives the objections real weight — this is playing favourites, and it distorts. It biases the market toward particular firms and technologies, it dampens the incumbent's incentive to invest while over-stimulating the entrant's, and there's a broad consensus it induces inefficient entry. Schmalensee named it, explicitly as a variant of the old infant-industry argument, and the infant-industry critique applies: temporary help has a habit of becoming permanent. Armstrong and Sappington's bottom line — the costs often exceed the benefits — is the sober practitioner's warning.

3.6.2 · REMOVING EX-ANTE PRICE CONTROLS

When is competition "effective enough" to let go?

Entry restrictions come off first; price controls come off later, and more gingerly. The regulator's nightmare is the middle ground — the two end-states are clear, everything between depends on context. Britain's regulators (via Beesley, for Ofgem) mapped a four-stage journey.

The two-sided risk. Hold price controls too long and you can hinder competition (a regulated price becomes a collusive focal point). Drop them too early and the incumbent exploits residual market power. As competition grows, the harm-risk falls and the distortion-risk rises — they cross.
This is the most operationally important slide in the module — it's what a UK energy or telecoms economist literally does. Removing entry restrictions is the easy half; removing price controls is the fraught half, and the whole difficulty is the middle. The two end-states — pure monopoly and effective competition — have clear characteristics and obvious regulatory answers. Everything in between is judgement. Michael Beesley's framework for Ofgem stylised it into four stages, which my step-chart traces as the incumbent's market power ratchets down. Stage one, pre-entry: incumbent near 100%, full price control. Stage two, pre-competitive: some entry but the incumbent is dominant or super-dominant on legacy advantages — controls stay. Stage three, established competition: the incumbent's power is materially reduced, entrants have reputations, and competition is now effective enough to restrain harm — this is where you can start withdrawing ex-ante price control. Stage four, effective competition: rivalry disciplines everyone, and the residual risk is no greater than any normal market, so ex-post competition law suffices. The genuinely hard bit is the callout's crossing risk: keep controls too long and the regulated price can become a collusive focal point that actually props prices up; pull them too soon and the incumbent gouges. Europe does periodic SMP assessments; the US lets firms that prove they lack market power charge market-based rates. Same problem, different machinery.

3.7 · NEGOTIATED SETTLEMENTS

Let the utility and its customers strike the deal.

Customers — via user and consumer groups — negotiate a settlement directly with the utility, then bring it to the regulator for approval, without a full hearing. Championed by Stephen Littlechild as an alternative to rate-of-return and RPI–X price caps. Live in US federal energy regulation, Canada, and states like Florida and California.

Why the regulator stays in the room. Unlike pure private contracting, a regulator still approves the deal, structures the negotiation, compels information disclosure, and decides through standard process if talks fail. It's a backstop, not an abdication.

The upside

Faster and cheaper than litigated proceedings; more flexible than one-size-fits-all; lets parties trade off issues as a package; better reflects consumer views; and — Littlechild's evidence — reaches outcomes the standard process wouldn't.

The catch: who's at the table?

Customer heterogeneity may be lost; consumer advocates may champion only "safe" issues (Gormley) or leave residential users worse off (Holburn & Spiller); and future consumers aren't present at all — costs can be deferred onto them.

Negotiated settlements are the alternative that's been gaining the most momentum lately, and Stephen Littlechild — the architect of RPI–X, interestingly — is its leading advocate. The idea inverts the adversarial regulatory hearing: instead of the regulator determining the price control after a technical proceeding, the utility and representatives of its customers sit down and negotiate a deal themselves, then take it to the regulator for a light-touch approval. It's established in US federal energy regulation, in Canada, and in states like Florida and California. The advantages Littlechild documents are real and attractive — it's faster, cheaper, avoids vast econometric battles, and lets parties trade across issues as a package rather than litigating each line item, and he finds it reaches more tailored outcomes than the litigated alternative. But note carefully why this isn't just deregulation-by-handshake: the regulator stays in the room as a backstop, structuring the talks, forcing disclosure, and deciding the old-fashioned way if agreement fails. The serious worry is representation — who actually sits at the table. Consumer heterogeneity gets flattened, advocates may pursue only safe revenue-requirement issues, and above all future consumers aren't there to defend themselves, so a deal that suits today's customers and the utility can quietly defer costs onto tomorrow's. That's the residual role for a regulator: to represent the absent.

3.8 · OTHER ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES

Two more, at the edges of the map.

3.8.1 · Private contracts

The most radical: rely on long-term private contracts enforced through ordinary commercial courts, not a regulator — arguably a stronger commitment device. Works for freight rail, airlines and large industrial customers of electricity/water (Gómez-Ibáñez).

Limit: impractical for many small customers with hard-to-specify needs; transaction costs can exceed those of regulation, where the regulator acts collectively for all buyers. Brokers or menus of standard contracts might help.

3.8.2 · Monitoring

A "light-handed" option: subject the utility to monitoring of prices and conduct under the standing threat of (re-)imposing regulation if behaviour disappoints. Used for airports in Australia and some UK airports.

Condition for success: absolute clarity on the criteria that trigger tougher regulation — otherwise the threat isn't credible.

The spectrum continues. Private contracting sits at the "least regulation" end (only commercial law applies); monitoring is a hair's breadth from regulation itself. Neither is a general solution — both are niche tools for specific circumstances.
Section 3.8 sweeps up two further alternatives that sit at the extreme ends of the interventionist spectrum. First, private contracts — the most radical option in the whole chapter. The idea is to strip the regulator out entirely and let utilities and customers write long-term private contracts enforced through the ordinary commercial courts. Gómez-Ibáñez argues, against intuition, that this can be a stronger commitment device than a regulator, because a court enforcing a contract is harder to lobby than an agency. It genuinely works in some corners — freight rail, airline services, and big industrial buyers of power and water who can look after themselves. The obvious limit is the small-customer problem: you can't write bespoke contracts with millions of households whose needs you can't even specify in advance, and Schmalensee's conclusion was that transaction costs make this impractical, which is exactly why you want a regulator acting as collective agent for all the buyers. Second, monitoring — a light-handed approach where you watch the utility's prices and conduct and hold regulation in reserve, ready to impose it if behaviour disappoints. Airports in Australia and the UK run on this. The critical success condition is clarity: the firm has to know precisely what would trigger tougher regulation, or the threat is empty. Both of these feed straight into the summary spectrum on the next slide.

The through-line

Regulation is one option among several — matched to the activity, not the industry.

SYNTHESIS · THE CONTROL SPECTRUM

From pure competition to state ownership.

The one idea to keep. There is no single answer. The right mechanism depends on the activity — sunk vs mobile capital, few vs many potential bidders, frequent vs occasional harm — so a modern utility is typically governed by several of these at once.
Let's pull the whole chapter together on one line. Lay the alternatives out from least to most intervention. At the far left, private contracts — pure commercial law, no regulator. Then contestability and ex-post competition law, where the market or the threat of entry does the disciplining. Then monitoring, then negotiated settlements, then franchise bidding, then traditional ex-ante regulation, and finally, at the most interventionist end, state ownership, where the government simply owns the thing. Here's the synthesis that matters and that Decker returns to again and again: you don't pick one point on this line for a whole industry. You pick per activity. The competitive layers — generation, retail, long-distance calls — can live at the left end, disciplined by competition or light-touch tools. The sunk, immobile core network — the wires and pipes — sits at the right end under full regulation because none of the market-based alternatives survive contact with large sunk costs. So a real modern utility is governed by several of these mechanisms simultaneously. The decision variables are the ones we've met all module: is capital sunk or mobile, are there many credible bidders or few, is potential harm frequent and irreversible or occasional. Get those right and the mechanism chooses itself.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is the decisive difference between franchise bidding and contestability?

A
Franchise bidding needs many bidders; contestability needs only one incumbent.

Both rely on freedom of entry to constrain the firm — the number of firms isn't the crux.

B
Franchise bidding grants an exclusive franchise to recover sunk costs over the contract; contestability assumes no sunk costs, so the entry threat alone disciplines the firm.

✓ Correct. The sunk-cost assumption is the dividing line. With no sunk costs you need no franchise at all; cost and technology conditions do the work.

C
Franchise bidding applies to networks; contestability applies to the whole economy.

Backwards — contestability is least applicable to sunk-cost networks; it fits mobile "capital on wheels."

Quick self-check, and it targets the single most examinable distinction in the chapter. Answer A is a plausible trap — it's true that franchising needs a competitive field of bidders while contestability can discipline even a lone incumbent, but that's a consequence, not the decisive mechanism; both approaches ultimately rely on freedom of entry to limit monopoly pricing. The correct answer is B, and it's worth saying out loud because Decker makes it explicitly: the dividing line is the treatment of sunk costs. Franchise bidding hands the winner an exclusive franchise precisely so it can recover its sunk investment over the life of the contract. Contestability assumes sunk costs are zero, which is exactly why you need no franchise — the cost and technology conditions alone keep the incumbent honest, because any misbehaviour invites instant hit-and-run entry. C is the reverse of the truth: contestability is least applicable to sunk-cost networks and most applicable to mobile capital. If a learner picks A, revisit slide 8's sunk-cost assumption; if C, revisit slide 9's capital-on-wheels distinction.
