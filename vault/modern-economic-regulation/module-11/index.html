the hardest utility to compete.

Water is heavy, local, quality-critical and bound to human health. That combination makes it the natural monopoly "par excellence" — the one industry where regulation is treated as permanent, not a bridge to competition. A tour of the physical economics, the England & Wales privatised model, RAB price controls, yardstick competition, gearing debates and conservation tariffs.

Welcome to Module 11 on water and wastewater. If you take one framing from this whole deck, take this: of all the utilities in Decker's book, water is the one where the case for competition is weakest and the case for permanent regulation strongest. The reasons are physical, not ideological — water is heavy so it's costly to move, its quality can't be judged by eye so trust matters enormously, and the whole thing is bound up with public health. So while telecoms and electricity restructured aggressively, water in most of the world stayed state-owned and vertically integrated. England and Wales is the great outlier — fully privatised in 1989, with an independent economic regulator, Ofwat, applying RPI–X style price caps and yardstick competition. We'll use that model as our worked example throughout, because it's the one that stress-tests every idea in the chapter: cost of capital, gearing, metering, affordability and conservation. CHAPTER 11 · WHY WATER IS DIFFERENT

The most essential service — and the least contestable.

Water and sanitation sit closer to human survival than any other utility. Quality can't be judged by sight, consumers can't switch supplier, and the externalities are severe. Hence: high state ownership, permanent regulation.

Health-critical

A contamination or sanitation failure has wide public-health impact. ~884m people lack safe water; ~2.6bn lack adequate sanitation (WHO).

Quality is opaque

You can't tell from a glass of water whether it's safe. Trust must be manufactured by regulation and testing, not by the market.

Big externalities

Leaks breed disease; upstream abstraction and effluent degrade downstream supply. Spillovers are pervasive and local.

The paradox of value. Adam Smith's diamond–water paradox: diamonds command a high price yet water is far more useful — you can live without one, not the other. That gap between price and value haunts every water tariff debate.
Start with what makes water sui generis. Decker opens by arguing that of all the utilities, water and wastewater services are arguably the most essential, precisely because of the intimate link to human health and survival. Three features flow from that. One, it's health-critical: a major failure — contamination, sanitation collapse — has widespread and potentially catastrophic effects, and the global access numbers are sobering, roughly 884 million without safe water and 2.6 billion without adequate sanitation. Famously, more people have a mobile phone than a toilet. Two, quality is opaque — you cannot look at water and know it's safe to drink, so the market can't police quality the way it polices, say, restaurant food. Three, the externalities are pervasive: leaky pipes create stagnant pools and disease vectors, and abstraction or effluent upstream degrades what everyone downstream can use. Add the EU framing — "water is not a commercial product like any other" — and Smith's paradox of value, and you see why water resists the market logic that reshaped telecoms.

11.1.1 · THE PRODUCT & ITS DEMAND

Not homogeneous, not storable at will, no substitutes.

Unlike electrons or gas molecules, raw water is heterogeneous — quality varies by source and location. And there are essentially no substitutes: you can switch fuels, but not water. Demand is seasonal, daily-peaked, and only somewhat price-responsive.

Water–electricity nexus. Just under half of all abstraction in the US and EU goes to cooling power plants; conversely the water industry uses large amounts of electricity for pumping. The two systems are deeply coupled.
Two economic facts about the product. First, raw water is heterogeneous — unlike a kilowatt-hour or a gas molecule, which are essentially identical once produced, raw water differs in quality by source and location, and it needs treatment to a level that matches its use. That's why the potable / non-potable distinction matters, and why reclaimed water can serve irrigation, cooling, street cleaning, even drinking in a few US counties like El Paso and parts of Orange County. Second, there are basically no substitutes — that's the key contrast with energy and telecoms, where you can switch fuels or technologies. The demand chart is worth a beat: households are the smallest slice everywhere, 5–8%. In the OECD industry dominates at about 65%; in developing countries agriculture dominates at around 69%. Demand is seasonal, peaks morning and evening, and is only modestly price-elastic — Noll's estimates run 0.3 to 0.7. And flag the water–electricity nexus: roughly half of abstraction goes to power-plant cooling, while pumping water consumes a lot of electricity. These systems are joined at the hip.

11.1.3 · THE WATER SUPPLY CHAIN

Abstraction → treatment → distribution → customer.

This is Decker's Figure 11.1, redrawn. Walk it left to right. Abstraction: raw water comes from two main sources — groundwater, drilled from aquifers and pumped up, and surface water from rivers, lakes and reservoirs. In the US most drinking water is surface; in Europe it's mostly groundwater. Treatment: groundwater needs less because the aquifer filters it naturally — often just chlorine — whereas surface water needs coagulation, sedimentation, filtration and disinfection. Storage in service reservoirs buffers peak demand. Then distribution — and here's the number to remember: roughly two-thirds of the total cost of supplying water sits in distribution. That's completely unlike electricity or gas, where transport is a small slice. Why? Water is heavy, it can't be compressed, moving it needs gravity plus a lot of pumping, and the buried pipe network is vast and old — 44% of London's mains are over a century old — with leakage of 10–20% in the OECD and far higher elsewhere. Retail — metering and billing — is a thin final layer, which matters later when we ask whether retail competition is even worth the transaction cost.

11.1.3 · THE WASTEWATER SUPPLY CHAIN

Collection → treatment → discharge (or reuse).

This is Decker's Figure 11.2. Wastewater mirrors water but runs the other way — most of what we consume becomes wastewater, so the two demands are tightly correlated. Stage one is collection: raw sewage enters through household sewers, drains and manholes. It comes in flavours — foul or domestic sewage from kitchens and bathrooms, trade effluent from industry, plus stormwater the operator usually also handles. Stage two, transport, shares all the natural-monopoly features of water distribution — sunk, immobile, long-lived pipes. Stage three, treatment, is where jurisdictions differ: England and Wales, Scotland and Canada treat mostly to secondary level, Australia mostly to tertiary, and the number of stages depends on the environmental standard for where the effluent will go. The four stages are preliminary, primary, secondary and tertiary. Note the outputs on the right — this is the modern, resource-recovery view. Effluent discharges to rivers or sea; treated water can be recycled for irrigation and industry; and sludge, once dumped at sea, is now a resource — fertiliser, biogas, even electricity fuel. Hold the "sewer mining" idea — extracting raw sewage to treat separately — because Box 11.1 turns it into an access-pricing puzzle.

Key terms

11.1.3 · STRUCTURE — SCALE, SCOPE & INTEGRATION

Fragmented systems, integrated firms — and no clear scale verdict.

Wildly varied fragmentation

USA: ~50,000 water systems (9% serve 90% of people) and ~50,000 wastewater systems. England & Wales: just 10 combined + 11 water-only. Scotland & N. Ireland: one company each.

Scale: no consensus

Surveys find no agreement on whether scale economies exist. The one robust finding: small firms could gain by growing, large firms may be past the optimum — a critical size (~100k–1m connections) after which economies exhaust.

Scope: also mixed

Evidence on integrating abstraction–treatment–distribution, and water with wastewater, is inconclusive — often positive for small firms only. Integration lets a firm internalise externalities (e.g. site treatment near discharge).

Integration is the norm

Full vertical integration in Chile, England & Wales, France, Italy and much of Europe. Water & wastewater usually combined — the Netherlands is the exception (10 water, 440+ wastewater firms).

Why it matters for the regulator. Separation improves the information a regulator sees and enables outsourcing — but sacrifices shared-cost efficiencies and the ability to internalise supply-chain externalities. The trade-off is genuinely finely balanced (Ch. 6).
Two structural observations. First, the number and size of systems varies enormously. The US has something like 50,000 drinking-water systems and 50,000 wastewater systems, but 9% of them serve 90% of the population — a long tail of tiny operators. Contrast England and Wales: ten combined water-and-wastewater companies plus eleven water-only, a deliberately consolidated structure. Scotland and Northern Ireland have literally one company each. Second, vertical integration is the dominant global model — full integration in Chile, England and Wales, France, Italy, much of Europe — and water is usually bundled with wastewater, the Netherlands being the striking exception. Now the economics: the survey evidence on scale economies reaches no consensus, but one robust pattern emerges — small companies would benefit from getting bigger while large ones may already be past their efficient size, implying a critical scale, somewhere between roughly 100,000 and a million connections, after which economies exhaust. Scope evidence is equally mixed. The practical upshot for a regulator: separation sharpens the information you get and enables outsourcing, but you lose shared-cost efficiencies and the firm's ability to internalise its own externalities. That trade-off recurs across the whole book.

Key terms

11.1.4 · TRADING ARRANGEMENTS FOR BULK WATER

Markets are thin — because water won't travel.

Wholesale trading is very limited. Supply is highly localised, interconnection is historically weak, and firms are vertically integrated — so scarcity is a local problem that markets struggle to arbitrage away.

Bulk water transfers

Neighbour-to-neighbour sales of raw or treated water — long-distance transfers in England & Wales; inter-regional wholesalers in Germany (~25% of delivered water); examples in Australia, France, USA.

Trading abstraction rights

Trading the licence to abstract — California, Colorado, Chile, Mexico, Australia. Often born of drought. California used government "water banks" that buy at an admin price and re-sell.

The core friction. Water is heavy and interconnection is limited, so you can't easily draw from a neighbouring region. Scarcity is localised — which is exactly why placing a market "value" on abstraction (section 11.3.5) is both attractive and hard.
Section 11.1.4 asks: why isn't there a wholesale water market like the ones in gas and electricity? The answer is physical again. Water is heavy and expensive to move, historic interconnection between systems is weak, and firms are vertically integrated — so there's little to trade and few counterparties. What trading exists comes in two forms. One, bulk transfers of raw or treated water between neighbours — England and Wales runs specific long-distance transfer schemes, and in Germany inter-regional wholesalers supply perhaps a quarter of delivered water, sometimes with genuine competition between sellers on different river basins. Two, and more interesting economically, trading the rights or licences to abstract water — seen in California, Colorado, Chile, Mexico and Australia, and usually triggered by drought. California organised this through government "water banks" that buy at an administratively set price then re-sell to farmers and cities. Chile and Australia rely more on genuine private market interactions. The takeaway to bank: because water won't travel, scarcity is inherently local, which is why putting a market value on abstraction — the subject of section 11.3.5 — is such a live and difficult reform.

Section 11.2.1 gives us the three organising models — memorise this table, it frames the whole regulatory discussion. The English model: assets are privately owned and operated, and an independent economic regulator supervises, leaning heavily on benchmarking or yardstick competition. England and Wales is the exemplar; Chile, Sweden and the Czech Republic are said to follow it. The French model: assets stay in public hands but private firms operate them under medium-to-long-term concession contracts awarded by tender — this is "competition for the market" from Chapter 3, and it's the single most common form of private-sector participation globally. It's "regulation by contract", though some jurisdictions add a supervisory agency for quality and surprises. The public-operator model: the state or municipality both owns and runs the system, and crucially there's often no separate economic regulator at all — the regulator, if any, is the environmental and quality body. This third model is the global default. So when we talk about Ofwat, RAB and yardstick competition, remember we're describing the English model — a minority arrangement worldwide, but the one that pushes every regulatory idea to its limit.

11.2.1 · WHAT'S CONTESTABLE, WHAT ISN'T

The pipes are a monopoly; the ends might not be.

Abstraction, treatment and retail don't inherently have natural-monopoly attributes. But distribution and sewerage transport do — sunk, immobile, single-purpose assets. So competition, where it exists, comes via access to the incumbent's network.

Abstraction could compete

Rival reservoirs/aquifers could compete — but blocked by sustainability, quality-blending & cross-subsidy worries.

Treatment could compete

Few scale economies; an entrant could treat under contract. "Sewer mining" recycles raw sewage separately.

Distribution monopoly

Sunk, immobile pipes. Competition only via common carriage / third-party access or inset appointments.

Retail could compete

Opened to non-household customers in E&W (all from 2017) & Scotland (2008). But value-added is tiny.

Bottom line. Direct competition in water is largely non-existent in most jurisdictions — reflecting limited prospects, high vertical integration, and quality/accountability concerns. There are essentially no deregulated activities, unlike other utilities.
Now map competition onto the supply chain. The key insight: the natural-monopoly bottleneck is narrow — it's the distribution pipes and the sewers, which are sunk, immobile and single-purpose. The activities at either end aren't inherently monopolistic. Abstraction could in principle be competitive — different operators drawing from competing reservoirs or aquifers and selling to a distributor, much like gas production. But it's been resisted, for three reasons: fear of over-exploiting the resource beyond sustainable levels, the quality and accountability mess of blending water from different sources into one pipe, and the unwinding of cross-subsidies that would spike some bills. Treatment could compete too — limited scale economies, so an entrant could treat under an outsourcing contract, and "sewer mining" is exactly that for sewage. Retail is contestable and has been opened to non-household customers in England and Wales and Scotland. But distribution is the genuine monopoly, so competition there only comes through access — common carriage, third-party access, or inset appointments. The honest summary Decker gives: direct competition is largely non-existent almost everywhere, and unlike other utilities there are no fully deregulated activities. Everything is either state-run or regulated.

BOX 11.1 · A REAL ACCESS DISPUTE

Valuing Sydney's sewage: the ECPR meets the sewer.

Services Sydney v Sydney Water — ACCC, 2007

Entrant Services Sydney sought access to incumbent Sydney Water's sewerage network: intercept raw sewage, pipe it to a new plant, treat it to tertiary level, then sell recycled water for non-potable use. Classic "sewer mining". The dispute forced the regulator (the ACCC) to choose an access-pricing method.

The ruling. The ACCC applied a retail-minus / "avoidable cost" form of the ECPR — access price = incumbent's retail price minus the costs it avoids by not serving that customer. But "avoidable cost" proved slippery: which assets count, historic vs replacement cost, which treatment technology, how efficiently optimised.

Two twists. (1) Retail sewerage prices were set on a geographically uniform ("postage-stamp") basis, so the ACCC kept access prices uniform too — de-averaging them would invite entrants to cherry-pick the cheapest-to-serve customers. (2) The "value" of the raw sewage itself was never resolved — and in the end Services Sydney walked away, so this novel ECPR application was never implemented.

Box 11.1 is a lovely, concrete application of Chapter 6's access-pricing theory. The setup: an entrant, Services Sydney, wanted to tap the incumbent Sydney Water's sewers, intercept raw sewage, pipe it to its own plant, treat it to a high tertiary level, and sell the recycled water. That's sewer mining. Because it needed access to the monopoly network, the regulator — here the ACCC, the same competition authority that does telecoms — had to set an access price. It chose a retail-minus version of the efficient component pricing rule: the access price equals what the incumbent charges at retail, minus the costs it avoids by not serving that customer. Sound clean in theory; in practice "avoidable cost" was a nightmare to pin down — which assets are relevant, do you value them at historic or replacement cost, which treatment technology do you assume. Two teaching points. First, because retail sewerage prices were uniform across the state — postage-stamp pricing — the ACCC deliberately kept access prices uniform too, otherwise entrants would cherry-pick the low-cost customers. Second, and delightfully, the deep question — what is raw sewage actually worth? — was dodged, and then Services Sydney abandoned the plan, so this elegant scheme never actually ran.

11.2.2 · FORM & SCOPE OF PRICE REGULATION

Two families — and the building-block sum.

Rate of return dominates globally — ~60% of regulating countries, incl. most US states (rate base, "used & useful", prudent cost). Price caps (RPI–X) were pioneered for water in England & Wales in 1989 as RPI − X + Q — the Q adding for quality investment.

Allowed revenue = the building blocks. Ofwat sets each firm's revenue as its Regulatory Capital Value times the cost of capital, plus operating cost and depreciation — then translates it into the K price limit for a 5-year period.
Section 11.2.2 covers the price-control machinery, and it's the same toolkit from Chapters 4 and 5 applied to water. Two families exist. Rate of return dominates the world — about 60% of regulating countries, including most US states, with the familiar rate base, "used and useful" test, and prudently-incurred costs. Price caps — RPI minus X — were actually pioneered for water in England and Wales in 1989, but with a twist: the formula is RPI minus X plus Q, where Q loads in the extra cost of meeting rising water-quality standards. The figure is the building-block calculation you must be able to draw. Allowed revenue equals the Regulatory Capital Value — in water it's called the RCV — multiplied by the weighted average cost of capital, giving the return on capital; plus operating cost; plus depreciation. Stack those three blocks and you get the revenue the firm is allowed, which Ofwat then converts into the K price limit set for a five-year period. Note the practical adaptation Littlechild proposed to make caps permanent: base the X reset on an industry yardstick, not the firm's own past, to blunt the ratchet effect. And the current control added a capital-expenditure menu — the CIS — to get firms to submit realistic business plans.

11.2.2 · COMPARATIVE COMPETITION

If you can't have rivals, compare the monopolists.

With no direct competition, the regulator manufactures a proxy: benchmark each firm against the others, especially the most efficient. This is the closest real-world case to Shleifer's yardstick model (Ch. 4).

Yardstick (price-setting)

E&W since 1989 — econometric & DEA models estimate efficient cost, then set expected productivity gains. Needs enough firms → a special merger regime guards comparators.

Hypothetical efficient firm

Chile & Peru set tariffs against a modelled ideal company assumed to meet demand efficiently.

Sunshine regulation

Australia, Brazil, Germany, Netherlands, Zambia — publish comparisons to "name & shame". In the Netherlands it lifted productivity and cut prices.

Diminishing returns. Ofwat now warns that yardstick gains have faded over time, and that comparative competition alone is unlikely to drive dynamic efficiency and innovation — hence ever-more-elaborate (and costly) benchmarking models.
Because you can't put two pipe networks head to head, the regulator engineers a substitute for competition: comparison. Yardstick, or comparative, competition benchmarks each firm against the others and especially against the most efficient performer, and England and Wales has used it since 1989. Decker notes this is the real-world application that comes closest to Shleifer's theoretical benchmarking model from Chapter 4. It's grown sophisticated — econometric models and data envelopment analysis, DEA, estimate how efficient each company could be, and that sets the productivity gain expected next period. Crucially it needs enough independent comparators, which is why England and Wales runs a special merger regime that restricts water mergers purely to preserve the yardstick — the competition authorities have blocked mergers on that ground alone. A variant used in Chile and Peru sets tariffs against a modelled hypothetical efficient firm. And sunshine regulation — used in Australia, Brazil, Germany, the Netherlands, Zambia — publishes the comparisons to name and shame, without directly setting prices; in the Netherlands it demonstrably raised productivity. The warning to carry forward: Ofwat itself now says the yardstick's returns have diminished, and that comparison alone won't deliver innovation — a theme in the policy section.

E&W regulators

11.2.2 · REGULATING QUALITY

Where you can't switch, quality must be policed.

Because customers can't choose supplier and can't judge safety by eye, quality is regulated on two tracks: dedicated health/environmental bodies, and the economic regulator — especially where firms are private and could trade quality for profit.

Dedicated quality regulators

In England & Wales: the Drinking Water Inspectorate for potable safety and the Environment Agency for abstraction & discharge. Separate from economic regulation.

Economic regulator does quality too

Ofwat builds quality into price-setting — a Service Incentive Mechanism adjusts the price limit by +0.5% to −1.0%, and each firm has a mandatory leakage target with fines for missing it.

Different tools elsewhere. Victoria (Australia): firms propose service standards and pay customer compensation if they miss. US PUCs rarely set reliability minimums but police pressure and aesthetics (colour, taste, odour).
Quality regulation takes on a special weight in water because of the life-and-health link and because two of our earlier facts collide: consumers can't switch supplier, and they can't judge safety by looking. So quality gets policed on two tracks. First, dedicated bodies — in England and Wales the Drinking Water Inspectorate handles potable safety and the Environment Agency handles abstraction and discharge, both separate from Ofwat. Second, and this is the interesting bit, the economic regulator also regulates quality, precisely because a private monopolist with no competitive discipline might be tempted to degrade service to lift profit. Ofwat bakes quality into the price control: for the 2015–2020 period a Service Incentive Mechanism adjusts each firm's allowed price limit within a band of plus 0.5% to minus 1% based on complaints and survey-measured customer experience, and every firm carries a mandatory annual leakage target with fines for missing it. Other jurisdictions use other levers — Victoria makes firms pay compensation directly to customers if they miss agreed standards, while US public utility commissions tend not to set reliability minimums but do police pressure and the aesthetics of colour, taste and odour.

11.3.1 · DID RESTRUCTURING WORK?

Twenty-five years on — a genuinely mixed verdict.

  • 1989 · Ten regional authorities privatised; Ofwat, NRA (now Environment Agency) & DWI created. Govt sets initial 10-yr price limits.
  • 1994 · Performance beat forecasts → regulator judged K-factors too generous and re-set prices early.
  • 1989–2011 · ~£90bn invested (£4.09bn/yr) → better drinking water, cleaner beaches, EU compliance.
  • Now · Bills up ~42% real (some studies) or flat/down (others); efficiency improved but gains diminishing; competition "unsatisfactory"; weak innovation incentives.
The honest scorecard. Investment and quality: clear wins. Prices: contested. Efficiency: up but slowing. Competition: a disappointment. Chile is judged a "success story"; Australian water trading added ~$220m to GDP in 2008–09.
Section 11.3.1 asks the practitioner's question: did England and Wales's near-unique experiment actually work? The timeline sets the frame. 1989: ten regional water authorities privatised, and the modern regulatory architecture — Ofwat, the National Rivers Authority now the Environment Agency, and the Drinking Water Inspectorate — created in one go. The government set generous initial price limits, and when the newly private firms outperformed, the regulator concluded the K-factors were too generous and reset prices early in 1994. Since then, reviews every five years. The big positive is investment — around £90 billion between 1989 and 2011, roughly £4 billion a year, and that is widely credited with cleaner drinking water, better beaches and EU compliance. But the rest is genuinely mixed. On prices, some studies say bills rose 42% in real terms, others say they fell — Decker deliberately reports both. Efficiency improved but the incremental gains are shrinking, which pushes the regulator toward ever more complex, costly benchmarking. And competition — inset appointments, common carriage — is judged a disappointment by regulator, incumbents and entrants alike. So: investment and quality, wins; prices and competition, contested. Chile, by contrast, is called a success story.

11.3.2 · OWNERSHIP — PRIVATE VS PUBLIC

Ownership doesn't settle it — but the balance sheet worries Ofwat.

Surveys are inconclusive on whether private beats public — US evidence finds private systems comply just as well and cost about the same or less; the E&W "experiment" shows heavy early investment but no clear productivity gain.

The real story is financial. A "flight from equity": E&W gearing rose from ~0% at privatisation to ~67% by March 2013, as listed firms gave way to institutional investors. Some firms now run 80% debt-to-equity.
Section 11.3.2 tackles the private-versus-public question head on, and the honest answer is: the evidence doesn't crown a winner. Surveys are inconclusive — some show public utilities outperforming, some the reverse, some too noisy to call. The US work, Wallsten and Kosec, finds privately owned systems comply with drinking-water rules just as well and cost consumers about the same or slightly less. And the English experiment shows heavy early investment — £17 billion in six years to meet EU standards — but crucially no clear improvement in productivity growth; some studies even find it slowed. So ownership alone doesn't settle performance. Where the chapter gets sharp is finance. There's been what Helm calls a flight from equity: at privatisation the firms carried essentially no debt, but by March 2013 average gearing had climbed to around 67%, well above Ofwat's notional 50%, as stock-market listings gave way to institutional investors, and some firms now run 80% debt to equity. That's the chart — gearing marching up and through the notional line. This raises Ofwat's real anxiety: are these highly leveraged structures sustainable, and do they load risk onto customers if a firm fails? That question is Box 11.2.

BOX 11.2 · THE CENTRAL CONTROVERSY

Cost of capital & the capital structure of E&W water.

Why gearing is the fight that never ends

The cost of capital sets the return the firm is allowed to recover, so it's the most contested number in the whole control. Regulators use the WACC:

WACC = Cost of debt × Gearing + Cost of equity × (1 − Gearing)

Cost of debt is easy (observe interest rates on the firm's bonds). Cost of equity is hard — regulators use the CAPM. E&W 2009 control: WACC 5.1%, on 57.5% gearing, 3.6% debt, 7.1% equity.

Does gearing even matter? Modigliani–Miller says no — cheaper debt is exactly offset by a costlier equity base, so firm value is independent of structure. But relax the assumptions — chiefly that debt interest is tax-deductible while dividends aren't — and gearing can lower the WACC. Ofwat therefore sets a single notional gearing, which risks tempting firms to "gear up" above it. Advisers to the 2014 review judged gearing not yet dangerous — but proposed lifting the notional band to 60–70%.

Box 11.2 is the intellectual heart of the chapter for a UK regulatory economist, so slow down. The cost of capital directly sets the return the firm recovers, which is why it's the single most fought-over number in any price control. Regulators estimate it as the weighted average cost of capital — the formula on screen — blending the cost of debt and the cost of equity by the gearing ratio. Estimating the cost of debt is easy: look at the interest on the firm's bonds. The cost of equity is the hard part, and regulators reach for the CAPM — risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium. The 2009 English control put the WACC at 5.1%. Then the deep question: does the debt-equity mix even affect the WACC? Modigliani–Miller says no — any saving from cheaper debt is precisely cancelled by the rising cost of a shrinking equity base, so firm value is independent of capital structure. But relax the assumptions, above all the fact that debt interest is tax-deductible while dividends are not, and gearing can genuinely lower the WACC. That's why Ofwat fixes a notional gearing rather than following actual structures — and why some argue firms then gear up above it to pocket the difference. The 2014 advisers said gearing wasn't yet dangerous, but tellingly proposed raising the notional band to 60–70%.

11.3.4 · WHY THE APPETITE REVIVED

Scarcity is reopening the competition question.

What's changed since the mid-2000s

Climate & scarcity: droughts forecast even where water was always ample. Population growth in dry areas (London). Experience of competition in energy/telecoms. Persistent entrants pursuing court access. Customer demand for choice.

What reform is proposed

Upstream abstraction-rights trading; extend retail to all non-household; reform common carriage (remove the ECPR "costs principle"); possible unbundling of the value chain — "no strong evidence against" it.

But it's political. The first E&W water regulator, Ian Byatt, "underestimated the instinctive as well as the interested opposition to competition in water in any form." Legal separation of retail arms (Cave review) was rejected by government.
Section 11.3.4 explains why, after two decades of "competition won't work here", the appetite revived around the mid-2000s. Several forces converged. The biggest is climate and scarcity — models predict more droughts over the next thirty to fifty years even in places that always had ample water, and urban population growth is piling demand onto already-scarce areas like London. Second, regulators now have real experience of unbundling and competition from energy and telecoms, so they're less afraid of it. Third, persistent would-be entrants kept pursuing court action to force network access, echoing Vogelsang's point that liberalisation almost always starts with entrepreneurs battering at entry barriers. And fourth, customers — especially business customers — increasingly want choice; surveys show most business customers support competition in principle. The reform menu is upstream abstraction trading, extending retail competition to all non-household users, reforming common carriage by removing the ECPR-based "costs principle", and possibly unbundling the value chain, on which a survey found "no strong evidence against". But the closing caution is essential: this is politically charged. Byatt, the first water regulator, candidly admitted he underestimated the instinctive opposition to water competition, and the government rejected Cave's recommendation to legally separate retail arms.

11.3.5 · WHOLESALE TRADING & THE "VALUE" OF WATER

Put a price on abstraction, and water flows to who values it.

Today, abstraction licences are priced administratively — reflecting management cost, not scarcity. So the "cost" of taking water ignores how scarce it is at that point — and in some E&W catchments water is over-abstracted.

The efficiency prize

~80% of OECD abstraction is agricultural, yet farmers value water less at the margin than other users. Trading reallocates raw water to higher-value uses — Australia's Murray–Darling added ~$220m to GDP.

The mixed record

Chile: efficiency & environmental gains, but later over-exploitation (Copiapó) & rights-hoarding by hydro firms. Colorado: fair but cumbersome. California water banks: over-purchased. Design of the "rights" is everything.

An E&W snag. Each firm has a single RCV covering all activities — and it sits ~80% below modern replacement value. To enable trading, that RCV must be disaggregated across the chain, which is hard and could raise the cost of capital and prices.
Section 11.3.5 is where scarcity meets pricing theory. Today, in most places including the US and England and Wales, the licence to abstract water is priced administratively, set to cover the regulator's management cost rather than the value of the water. The consequence is that the cost of taking water carries no signal about how scarce it is at that point — and in several English catchments the result is over-abstraction that threatens sustainability. The reform idea is to let abstraction rights trade, so water flows to whoever values it most. The efficiency prize is large: roughly 80% of OECD abstraction goes to agriculture, yet farmers typically place a lower marginal value on water than industrial or urban users, so reallocation creates value — Australia's Murray–Darling trading added about $220 million to GDP in one year. But the record is genuinely mixed: Chile got efficiency and environmental gains but later over-exploitation in the Copiapó basin and rights-hoarding by hydro companies; Colorado's system is fair but cumbersome; California's government water banks over-purchased. The overarching lesson is that efficacy depends entirely on how the rights and institutions are designed. And note the peculiarly English obstacle: each firm has one combined RCV, sitting some 80% below modern replacement value, which would have to be painstakingly disaggregated to enable trading — potentially raising the cost of capital.

11.3.6 · INVESTMENT & NETWORK INTEGRATION

Ageing pipes, scarce water — and the case for a "water grid".

Replacing ageing water/wastewater infrastructure is estimated at ~$200bn/yr for industrial countries. The US EPA put its 20-year need at $334.8bn just to meet health standards. Climate adds desalination, bigger dams and recycling on top.

The interconnection idea

Build "water grids" that move water between regions like electricity or gas grids. Australia's Sugarloaf pipeline (70+ km) links the Murray–Goulburn and Melbourne grids.

Why it can pay

Interconnection can substitute for very costly new sources. A 2011 E&W White Paper estimated grid savings up to £960m over asset lifetimes; the Water Act 2014 makes inter-company trading easier.

The link back. Grids raise the same transfer/trading issues as section 11.3.5 — but where water can move between regions, localised scarcity eases and a market for bulk water becomes thinkable.
Section 11.3.6 is about capital — the sheer scale of investment water needs, and a structural fix for scarcity. The numbers are eye-watering: replacing ageing infrastructure runs to roughly $200 billion a year across industrial countries, the US EPA estimated $334.8 billion over twenty years just to keep water networks compliant with health standards, and one industry consortium put the US figure above a trillion dollars. On top of the maintenance bill, climate response adds desalination plants, enlarged dams and recycling projects. The more interesting idea economically is interconnection — building water grids that shift water between regions the way electricity and gas grids do. Australia's Sugarloaf pipeline, over 70 kilometres, links the Murray–Goulburn grid to Melbourne's. The reason a regulator likes this: interconnection can substitute for extremely costly investment in brand-new water sources. England and Wales's 2011 water White Paper estimated savings up to £960 million over the assets' lifetimes, and the Water Act 2014 makes it easier for companies to buy and sell water from each other. And notice the connection back to the previous slide — grids and trading are two sides of the same coin. Once water can physically move between regions, the localised scarcity that killed wholesale markets starts to ease, and a genuine bulk-water market becomes conceivable.

11.3.7 · AFFORDABILITY, EFFICIENCY & DEMAND

If you don't meter, the marginal price of water is zero.

The three-way tension

Prices must recover cost, stay affordable, and signal scarcity. Historically set too low on affordability grounds → over-consumption. Hence the global push to full-cost pricing (incl. environmental & resource costs — EU WFD).

The metering problem

Many E&W households pay by rateable (property) value, not usage — meter penetration was just 30% in 2008. No meter → variable price effectively zero → no conservation signal. Walker (2009) urged switching to metered, volumetric charging.

Externalities tip the scales. Metering's private benefits often don't beat its install-and-read cost (10–15% of a bill). But once you count the social cost of over-use, the case for metering strengthens — especially where water is scarce.
Section 11.3.7 brings us to pricing, the issue every water regulator lives with regardless of ownership. There's a three-way tension: prices need to recover full cost, stay affordable, and signal scarcity so people don't waste water. Historically, affordability won — prices were set below cost on political grounds, which encouraged over-consumption and ignored environmental costs entirely. So there's a worldwide movement toward full-cost pricing, and in the EU the Water Framework Directive explicitly requires recovery of environmental and resource costs, not just operating cost. But here's the structural snag that's peculiarly British: in England and Wales many households aren't metered at all — they pay by the rateable value of their property — and meter penetration was only about 30% in 2008. If there's no meter, the variable price of water is effectively zero, so there is literally no marginal incentive to conserve. The traditional argument against metering was cost — installing and reading meters can be 10–15% of a bill, and water's fixed-to-variable cost ratio is high anyway. But that calculation only weighs private benefits. Once you add the social cost of over-use — the externality — the scales tip toward metering, especially where water is scarce. Walker's 2009 review recommended exactly that switch to volumetric charging, plus exploring smart meters.

11.3.7 · TARIFF STRUCTURE FOR CONSERVATION

Flip the price schedule to punish heavy use.

The old logic charged less per unit as you used more (a decreasing block tariff — reflecting cost efficiencies). To conserve, US utilities are switching to increasing block tariffs: each successive block costs more per unit.

The regulator's dilemma. Loading cost onto the variable charge signals conservation to customers — but gives the firm a perverse incentive to sell more water. Fix: decoupling / revenue-adjustment mechanisms that break the sales–revenue link.
This is the conservation-tariff mechanics, and the figure is the increasing block tariff you must be able to sketch. Historically water utilities used decreasing block tariffs — the more you consumed, the less you paid per unit — justified by the cost efficiencies of serving a big customer, which is a legitimate efficient-pricing argument if marginal cost really falls. But to conserve water you want the opposite. So many US utilities, especially in dry states like California, Arizona, Nevada and Florida, have moved to increasing block tariffs: the first block, often a subsistence block, is cheap or even free, and each successive block costs more per unit, so heavy users pay a rising marginal price. That's the staircase on screen climbing left to right. Two design issues. First, affordability — if the first block ignores household size, a large, often poorer, family gets pushed into the expensive tiers, so blocks should reflect household size and ability to conserve. Second, and this is the neat regulatory paradox in the callout: loading cost onto the variable charge tells customers to conserve, but it simultaneously gives the firm an incentive to sell more water, because that's where its revenue now comes from. The fix is decoupling — revenue-adjustment mechanisms, used in California, that sever the link between water sales and firm revenue.

BOX 11.3 · A THREE-WAY BALANCING ACT

Increasing block tariffs in the US water industry.

Balancing affordability, revenue adequacy & conservation

Two roles, two regions. In developing economies, an increasing block tariff sets a below-cost (even free) subsistence first block so poor households can afford basic water, with richer, higher-using households cross-subsidising them. In the US, the same structure is used chiefly to encourage conservation.

The US shift. Decreasing block tariffs fell from ~60% of US tariffs (1982) to ~45% (1992); California's use of increasing block rose from 27% of utilities (1991) to 43% (2006), spurred by a 2009 law targeting a 20% per-capita cut by 2020. Evidence: customers do respond — average and peak demand fall.

Two catches. (1) Affordability — if the first block ignores household size, big (often poorer) households pay a higher average price; California tailors blocks and the CPUC checks low-income impact. (2) Revenue volatility — conservation cuts sales, so revenue swings; hence revenue-adjustment / decoupling mechanisms.

Box 11.3 ties the pricing thread together and shows the same instrument doing two different jobs in two different worlds. In developing and transitional economies, the increasing block tariff is fundamentally a social tool: the first block, the subsistence block, is priced below cost or free so the poorest households can afford basic water, and the assumption is that richer households use more and therefore cross-subsidise the poor. But watch the caveat Decker flags — Estache and Wren-Lewis find the income-consumption correlation for water is weaker than for electricity, and the OECD notes these tariffs can be regressive where the poor aren't even connected to the network, so the subsidy misses them. In the US, the same staircase structure is used mainly to drive conservation, and it's spreading fast — California's use of increasing block tariffs rose from 27% of utilities in 1991 to 43% by 2006, pushed by a 2009 law targeting a 20% per-capita reduction by 2020, and the evidence shows customers genuinely respond, with average and peak demand falling. Two catches to remember: the affordability trap, where ignoring household size punishes large poor families, which California handles by tailoring blocks and having the CPUC vet low-income impact; and revenue volatility, because conservation cuts sales, which is why decoupling mechanisms are needed to protect the utility's cost recovery.
