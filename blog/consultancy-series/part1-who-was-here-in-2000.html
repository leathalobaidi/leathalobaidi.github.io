Who Was Here in 2000

Part 1 of 7: the institutions and firms from which the modern trade grew.

Leath · Revised November 2026

Correction, 14 November 2026. The July edition described the sum of the selected accounts as a UK turnover floor. The financial figures combine different activities, geographies and reporting periods and cannot establish a floor for UK economics consultancy. Revision notes.

In January 2000, Frontier Economics was less than a year old. RBB had yet to open; the Competition and Markets Authority would not assume its main functions until April 2014. The business of selling economic advice was already established. NERA, London’s Lexecon Ltd, LECG and Oxera illustrate the mix of regulatory, competition and litigation expertise from which it developed.

Those four firms provide a starting cast, not a census. Around them were macroeconomic forecasters, policy specialists and younger consultancies. Their histories suggest two broad origins for the trade: the demand for forecasts, and the demand for advice about how markets should work.

From institutes to subscriptions

The National Institute of Economic and Social Research, founded in 1938, predates both strands of the post-war story. The Institute for Fiscal Studies followed in 1969. Neither fits neatly into a commercial consultancy league table. Their independent research belongs in the institutional background to the trade. John Kay’s later move from IFS to found London Economics supplies one specific career connection.

University forecasting groups provided another route. At London Business School, Sir James Ball helped develop computerised macroeconomic forecasting. Academic models could also underpin a commercial product. Oxford Economic Forecasting made its global model available to clients in 1982, a year after its foundation.

John Walker founded Oxford Economic Forecasting, now Oxford Economics, in 1981. Cambridge Econometrics was incorporated in 1985; its modelling carried forward work from Cambridge’s Growth Project. Tim Congdon established Lombard Street Research in 1989. Cebr’s current company was incorporated in March 1991, while its founder dates the commercial firm to 1993. Roger Bootle launched Capital Economics in 1999.

These firms sold an interpretation of the economy to many subscribers. Competition and regulatory consultants generally worked on a particular client's problem. The distinction matters later: a subscription business and an expert-witness practice need not attract the same owners or reward their economists in the same way.

Privatisation creates a different client

The regulatory strand had American antecedents. Jules Joskow and Irwin Stelzer founded NERA in New York in 1961, building on work in gas-pricing litigation. Its London business also predates the current UK company. Derek Ridyard spent fifteen years in NERA’s London office before co-founding RBB in 2002; NERA UK Limited was incorporated in April 2000. A registration date alone is a poor guide to a consultancy’s arrival.

Privatisation changed the questions facing utilities. A regulated company needed to understand the prices it could charge, the investment it could recover and the terms on which competitors might use its network. Stephen Littlechild’s 1983 report on BT supplied a prominent example: the RPI–X price cap, which linked permitted price increases to inflation less an efficiency factor.

Oxera dates its founding to 1982. Its regulation, finance and competition work put it close to these questions. John Kay established London Economics in 1986. Simon Gaysford and Daniel Elliott were among Frontier’s founders in 1999; Part 2 follows that firm’s choice of ownership.

Four established names

NERA brought international experience. Derek Ridyard had led its European competition practice. He, Simon Bishop and Simon Baker worked in the NERA competition team before founding RBB in 2002. Their departure is central to this series because they built a specialist rival together.

Lexecon Ltd requires more care. The London company’s first full accounts say it began trading on 1 February 1991. The company information page lists Cento Veljanovski and Bill Bishop as directors. By the time of CRA’s proposed acquisition in 2005, Bishop was its chairman. It was distinct from Chicago’s Lexecon Inc. The Chicago firm’s 1977 founders were Richard Posner, William Landes and Andrew Rosenfield; Daniel Fischel and Dennis Carlton joined later. CRA’s UK subsidiary bought the London company’s equity in 2005. In 2003, FTI bought substantially all of the American business’s assets and assumed certain liabilities.

LECG, founded in California in 1988 by David Teece and colleagues, combined consulting work with academic experts. It later built a European competition practice under Jorge Padilla. Its eventual collapse, and the transfer of that practice, belong to Part 3.

Oxera offered a domestically founded alternative. Colin Mayer chaired the Oxford firm from 1986 to 2010. Its account of the 2003 management buyout identifies Helen Jenkins and Luis Correia da Silva as the joint managing directors who led its next stage. The same account dates its conversion to an LLP to 2014.

The work behind the firms

Britain’s competition system included the Office of Fair Trading and Competition Commission, alongside sector regulators. The CMA assumed its principal functions in April 2014. Those institutions supplied work for economists and, in cases such as John Fingleton’s later move into consultancy, experienced advisers. Their existence alone does not tell us how much consultancy work they generated.

The Airtours case shows why the analysis mattered. In 1999 the European Commission blocked Airtours’ proposed acquisition of First Choice, arguing that it risked collective dominance: firms could coordinate their conduct without an explicit agreement. In 2002 the Court of First Instance annulled the decision. The economics behind a merger prohibition had to withstand examination in court.

Such cases help explain the commercial opportunity. A contested merger can require specialists to test a theory, analyse data and explain conclusions to lawyers, regulators and judges. That is an inference about demand for expertise; the judgment does not identify each consultancy’s role or earnings.

Several ways to sell advice

The year-2000 picture already contains several business models: international subsidiaries, independent specialists, subscriptions and employee ownership. What changes over the following decades is their scale, their ownership and the firms through which senior economists move.

The current catalogue follows 90 firms associated with the trade. It cannot be compared with these four selected predecessors as a measure of market growth. The history is more specific: people left established firms, created new ones and chose who would own the proceeds.
