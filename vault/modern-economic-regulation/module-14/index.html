THEME 1 · FOUNDATIONS — MARKET STRUCTURE

Why utilities are special.

TermDefinition
Natural monopolyOne firm supplies the whole market more cheaply than several could.
SubadditivityA single firm's cost of total output is below the sum of any split among firms — the true test of natural monopoly.
Economies of scaleAverage cost falls as output rises.
Economies of scopeCheaper to produce two goods together than separately.
Economies of densityUnit cost falls as more customers share the same fixed network.
Sunk costsSpending that cannot be recovered on exit; a barrier to entry and to contestability.
SustainabilityA monopoly's ability to hold prices that deter entry without losing money.
Cream-skimmingEntrants target only the profitable customers, undermining averaged prices.
Hold-upOne party under-invests, fearing the other will later exploit its sunk commitment.
This cluster is the bedrock — taught in Modules 01 and 02 — and it matters because everything downstream assumes it. The examinable trap is the difference between economies of scale and subadditivity: scale is about a single product's falling average cost, whereas subadditivity is the real, multi-product definition of natural monopoly and can hold even when the cost curve turns upward. Economies of scope and density explain why you don't want duplicate pipes and why a dense city network is cheaper per customer than a rural one. Sunk costs are the hinge of the whole subject — they're what makes entry hard, contestability weak, and hold-up possible. Cream-skimming and sustainability go together: if an incumbent averages prices across profitable and unprofitable customers, an entrant can skim the profitable ones, so the incumbent's price structure must be entry-proof to survive. Hold-up previews the commitment problem you'll meet again under Institutions. Get these nine cold; they recur constantly.

THEME 1 · FOUNDATIONS — EFFICIENCY & WELFARE

The three efficiencies, and the welfare pie.

TermDefinition
Allocative efficiencyOutput where price equals marginal cost; no gains from trade left unrealised.
Productive efficiencyProducing at least cost for the output chosen.
Dynamic efficiencyEfficient innovation and investment over time.
X-inefficiencySlack: costs above the minimum because monopoly removes competitive pressure.
Cost efficiencyUmbrella term for producing output at minimum feasible cost.
Deadweight lossWelfare destroyed when price above MC cuts output below the efficient level.
Consumer surplusValue consumers get above the price they pay.
Producer surplusRevenue a firm earns above its marginal costs.
Total surplusConsumer plus producer surplus; the welfare pie regulators maximise.
This is the analytical heart of the course, from Modules 01 and 02. The three efficiencies are the criteria against which every regulatory decision is judged, and the figure shows why they don't always pull together. Allocative efficiency is a static, this-period idea: set price equal to marginal cost and you exhaust all gains from trade. Productive efficiency asks whether, for whatever you produce, you produce it at least cost — this is where X-inefficiency bites, the managerial slack a monopoly can afford because nobody is competing it away. Dynamic efficiency is the long game: innovation and timely investment, which sometimes require the very margins that hurt allocative efficiency in the short run. That tension — a fat margin funds tomorrow's investment but destroys today's deadweight — is the recurring dilemma of incentive regulation. The surplus vocabulary underneath is your measuring tape: consumer surplus, producer surplus, and their sum, total surplus, which is the pie a welfarist regulator tries to maximise. Deadweight loss is simply the slice that vanishes when price sits above marginal cost.

Module 02

THEME 1 · FOUNDATIONS — EXTERNALITIES & NETWORKS

When the market misses a cost or a value.

TermDefinition
ExternalityA cost or benefit falling on third parties, not priced by the market.
Environmental externalityPollution and emissions costs borne by society — e.g. electricity CO₂.
Network externalityA user's value rises as more others join the same network.
Positive / negative network externalityJoining benefits others (positive) or congests / harms them (negative).
Complementary servicesServices worth more together, often across adjacent monopolies.
Double-marginalisationSuccessive mark-ups by a vertical chain push price above the monopoly optimum.
Why it belongs in regulation. Externalities are the second market failure (after natural monopoly) that justifies intervention — and network externalities are what make telecoms and payments markets tip toward a dominant standard.
These six close out the foundations, from Module 02, and they broaden the case for regulation beyond pure monopoly. An externality is any cost or benefit the market price doesn't capture — the classic one in utilities is environmental, the CO₂ from generation that society pays for but the generator historically didn't. Network externalities are subtler and hugely important for telecoms: the value of a phone or a payment network rises with the number of users, which can make markets tip toward a single standard and hand the winner durable market power. Distinguish positive from negative carefully — an extra user on a social network helps everyone; an extra car on a road congests it. Complementary services and double-marginalisation both foreshadow the access chapters: when two separately-owned monopolies each mark up a product that must be consumed together, the combined price ends up higher than a single integrated monopolist would set — the double-marginalisation problem — which is a surprisingly strong argument for either integration or coordinated access pricing.

THEME 2 · WHY WE REGULATE — RATIONALES & THEORIES

Normative should vs positive does.

TermDefinition
Economic regulationRules constraining a firm's prices, conduct or entry to serve the public interest.
Normative theoryRegulation ought to correct market failure (natural monopoly, externalities).
Positive theoryExplains what regulation actually is, and whose interests it serves.
Public-interest rationaleRegulate to lift welfare where competition cannot.
Capture theoryRegulators come to serve the industry they oversee, not consumers.
Interest-group theoryRegulation is the outcome of groups lobbying for rents (Stigler, Peltzman, Becker).
Regulation as long-term contractA durable, adjustable bargain between state and firm under uncertainty.
Destructive competitionThe (weak) claim that unregulated rivalry is ruinously wasteful.
This cluster, from Modules 02 and 03, is where the subject gets intellectually honest. Hold the normative–positive distinction firmly: normative theory tells you what regulation should do — fix market failures — while positive theory, associated with Chicago economists Stigler, Peltzman and Becker, asks the uncomfortable empirical question of what regulation actually does and who really benefits. Capture theory and interest-group theory are the sharp end of the positive tradition: the worry that a regulator, over time, ends up defending the incumbents it was meant to discipline, or that the whole apparatus is just the equilibrium of lobbying. "Regulation as long-term contract" is a more constructive framing — a durable, renegotiable bargain between the state and the firm that has to survive shocks neither party foresaw, which is why credible commitment matters so much later. Destructive competition is flagged as weak deliberately: the claim that rivalry is ruinous has been used to justify cartels and is usually a bad-faith argument. Teach learners to be sceptical of it.

THEME 3 · PRICING PRINCIPLES — THE BENCHMARKS

Getting prices right in a monopoly.

First-best rule. Set p = MC — allocatively perfect, but a natural monopoly with falling average cost then loses money. Every other rule below is a fix for that break-even problem.
TermDefinition
Marginal-cost / first-best pricingPrice = MC; allocatively efficient but loss-making for a natural monopoly.
Second-best pricingBest achievable prices once the break-even (or other) constraint binds.
Average-cost pricingPrice = average cost; breaks even but distorts consumption.
Linear pricingA single per-unit price with no fixed element.
Cross-subsidyProfits on one service fund below-cost pricing of another.
Transfer subsidyAn explicit payment used to keep a loss-making service viable.
Price discriminationCharging different prices to different buyers by willingness to pay.
Demand-reflective pricingPrices that track how much each user values the service.
Now we're into the pricing toolkit, taught across the pricing modules. The organising idea is the one in the callout: the textbook-perfect rule is price equals marginal cost, but for a natural monopoly with falling average cost, marginal cost sits below average cost, so pricing at MC means running a loss. That single fact generates the entire rest of the theme. Second-best pricing is the honest response — the best you can do once you accept the firm must at least break even. Average-cost pricing is the crude fix: it balances the books but distorts consumption because price no longer equals marginal cost. The remaining terms are the finer instruments. Cross-subsidy and transfer subsidy are how loss-making services get funded — implicitly through other customers, or explicitly through a payment. Price discrimination and demand-reflective pricing are the bridge to the next slide: charging by willingness to pay isn't just a profit trick, it's often the welfare-improving way to recover fixed costs, which is precisely what Ramsey pricing formalises.

THEME 3 · PRICING — RAMSEY & MULTI-PRODUCT RULES

Recovering fixed cost with least harm.

TermDefinition
Ramsey–Boiteux pricingWelfare-optimal break-even prices: inverse-elasticity mark-ups.
EPMUEqui-proportionate mark-up: same proportional mark-up on every service — simpler than Ramsey.
FDCFully distributed costs: accounting method allocating common costs by a chosen key.
Common / joint costsCosts shared across services that no single output causes.
Stand-alone costCost of producing a service entirely on its own — a price ceiling test.
Incremental costExtra cost of adding one service to the mix — a price floor test.
Ramsey–Boiteux is the crown jewel of regulatory pricing theory, and the figure shows exactly where it sits: it's the second-best answer to the break-even problem the previous slide set up. The intuition worth drilling is the inverse-elasticity rule — to recover fixed costs with the least loss of welfare, load the biggest mark-ups onto the services whose demand is least elastic, because those buyers cut back least when the price rises. EPMU is the practical, blunt cousin: mark everything up by the same proportion, which is easy to defend but ignores elasticities. The cost concepts underneath are the guardrails for any multi-product price. Common or joint costs are the awkward shared costs no single output causes, and how you allocate them is contestable — fully distributed costs is one accounting convention. Stand-alone cost and incremental cost are the two tests that bound a legitimate price: no service should be charged more than it would cost to provide entirely on its own (the ceiling), and none should be charged less than the extra cost it adds (the floor). Those two bounds reappear, renamed, in the access-pricing slides.

THEME 3 · PRICING — TARIFFS & TIME

Peak-load, two-part and block tariffs.

TermDefinition
Two-part tariffA fixed access charge plus a per-unit price; recovers fixed cost while pricing usage near MC.
Non-linear pricingPer-unit price varies with quantity (blocks, declining rates).
Optimal non-linear pricingQuantity-varying schedule that can raise welfare as a Pareto improvement.
Block tariffPrice steps by consumption band (increasing or decreasing block).
Peak-load pricingHigher prices in high-demand periods to reflect capacity costs.
Time-of-use / dynamic tariffPrices that vary by time of day or real-time conditions.
Ramsey vs peak-loadRamsey rations by elasticity; peak-load rations scarce capacity by period.
Sustainable pricesA price set that covers cost and deters inefficient entry.
These eight are the practical face of the pricing theory, and they're the tariffs your electricity or water bill actually uses. The two-part tariff is the elegant one: a fixed charge recovers the fixed cost while the per-unit price stays near marginal cost, so you get the fixed-cost recovery of average-cost pricing without the consumption distortion — as close to first-best as a break-even monopoly can get. Non-linear and block tariffs generalise that idea to menus where the per-unit price changes with how much you buy; done well, an optimal non-linear schedule can make everyone better off, a genuine Pareto improvement. Peak-load pricing is conceptually distinct and worth contrasting explicitly with Ramsey, which is why it's flagged: Ramsey rations by elasticity to cover fixed costs, whereas peak-load rations scarce capacity by time period — you pay more at 6pm not because your demand is inelastic but because that's when the system is strained. Time-of-use and dynamic tariffs are the smart-meter-era implementation of peak-load, and they're central to the modern electricity modules.

THEME 4 · PRICE CONTROL — RATE OF RETURN

The old US model: cost-of-service.

TermDefinition
Rate-of-return (cost-of-service) regulationAllow prices covering costs plus a fair return on the asset base.
Rate baseThe capital value on which the allowed return is earned.
Rate caseThe periodic proceeding that sets allowed revenue.
Revenue requirementTotal revenue a firm is permitted: operating costs + return on rate base.
Averch–Johnson effectIf the allowed return exceeds cost of capital, the firm over-invests in capital ("gold-plating").
Cost-plus contractPays realised costs plus a margin; weak efficiency incentive.
Regulatory lagThe gap between reviews; longer lag sharpens cost-cutting incentives.
The core weakness. Cost-plus rewards spending, not thrift — the Averch–Johnson gold-plating incentive — which is exactly what RPI–X was invented to fix.
This is the classic American model, and it's the foil against which the British incentive model is defined. Under rate-of-return regulation, the regulator lets the firm charge prices that cover its costs plus a "fair" return on its rate base — the capital it has invested. The mechanics are the rate case, a periodic proceeding, which sets the revenue requirement: operating costs plus the allowed return. The problem is baked in and examinable: the Averch–Johnson effect. If the allowed rate of return is set even slightly above the firm's true cost of capital, the firm makes money on every pound of capital it deploys, so it over-invests — gold-plating the network. More broadly, cost-plus rewards spending rather than thrift, so efficiency incentives are weak. The one redeeming feature is regulatory lag: because reviews are periodic, a firm that cuts costs between reviews keeps the savings until the next reset, which quietly restores some incentive to be efficient. That insight — that the length of the review period is itself an incentive lever — is the seed of price-cap regulation on the next slide.

THEME 4 · PRICE CONTROL — INCENTIVE (CAP) & BENCHMARKING

The British model, and how targets are set.

TermDefinition
Price-cap regulationCap prices for a set period; the firm keeps savings it makes below the cap.
RPI − XCap letting prices rise with inflation minus an efficiency factor X.
X factorThe annual real price reduction the regulator expects from efficiency.
RAB / RCVRegulatory asset base / capital value: the regulated value of assets on which returns accrue.
Building-blocks approachAllowed revenue = opex + return on RAB + depreciation + tax.
Tariff-basket capCap on the weighted average of a bundle of prices, not each one.
Revenue capCap on total revenue, insulating the firm from volume risk.
Ratchet effectFirms hide efficiency, fearing the regulator claws it back next period.
Sharing & benchmarking
Profit-sharing (sliding scale)Customers and firm split gains/losses beyond a band.
DeadbandThe range of outcomes left unshared before sharing kicks in.
Menu regulationOffer a choice of contracts so the firm self-reveals its costs.
Yardstick competitionSet allowed cost from the performance of comparable peers.
BenchmarkingCompare firms' costs / efficiency to set targets.
DEAData envelopment analysis: non-parametric efficiency frontier.
COLSCorrected ordinary least squares: regression-based frontier.
SFAStochastic frontier analysis: separates inefficiency from noise.
TFPTotal factor productivity: output-to-all-inputs ratio; drives X.
This is the densest slide, and deliberately so — it's the machinery a UK regulator like Ofgem or Ofwat actually operates, so for practitioners it's the most useful cluster in the deck. The left column is the price-cap system. RPI–X is the headline: prices may rise with retail inflation minus an efficiency factor X, and because the firm keeps whatever it saves below the cap during the period, it has a genuine incentive to cut costs — the fix for Averch–Johnson. The RAB, or regulatory capital value, is the single most important number in British regulation: the regulated asset base on which the firm earns its return, built up through the building-blocks approach of opex plus return on RAB plus depreciation plus tax. The ratchet effect is the flaw — if firms fear this period's efficiency becomes next period's tougher target, they hide it. The right column is how the regulator sets X credibly: yardstick competition and benchmarking against peers, using frontier methods — DEA, COLS, SFA — to estimate how efficient each firm could be, and menu regulation to make firms reveal their true costs. TFP growth ultimately anchors the X factor.

THEME 5 · ACCESS — BASICS & PRICING RULES

Sharing the bottleneck network.

Access basics
AccessA rival's right to use an incumbent's essential network.
One-way accessEntrant buys access into a monopoly network to reach customers.
Two-way accessInterconnection: networks terminate each other's traffic; each needs the other.
Essential facilityAn input a rival must have and cannot economically duplicate.
Competitive bottleneckControl of a must-have facility conferring market power.
Access pricingThe charge for using another firm's network.
Retail-minusAccess price = retail price minus the incumbent's avoided retail cost.
Opportunity cost of accessProfit the incumbent forgoes by carrying a rival's traffic.
Vertical integration / separationWhether the network owner also competes downstream, or is split off.
Access pricing rules
ECPR / Baumol–WilligAccess price = incremental cost + opportunity cost of access.
M-ECPRMarket-adjusted ECPR: benchmarked to competitive, not monopoly, margins.
LRICLong-run incremental cost of the access service.
TSLRIC / TELRICTotal-service / total-element LRIC; forward-looking cost standards.
SACStand-alone cost: the ceiling for an access charge.
FL-LRICForward-looking LRIC, priced on efficient modern-equivalent assets.
Make-or-buyEntrant's choice to build its own facility or buy access.
By-passA rival routes around the incumbent's network, constraining access prices.
Access is the hardest and most modern problem in the book, and this slide is its vocabulary core. The basic set on the left frames the situation: an incumbent owns an essential facility — a bottleneck the rival cannot economically duplicate — and regulation forces it to grant access. One-way access is an entrant renting into a monopoly network; two-way access, or interconnection, is when two networks must terminate each other's traffic and neither can do without the other. The pricing rules on the right are where the intellectual fights happen. ECPR, the Baumol–Willig rule, says the access price should equal the incremental cost of carrying the rival plus the opportunity cost the incumbent bears by losing that retail sale — controversial because it can lock in the incumbent's existing margin, which is why M-ECPR benchmarks to competitive margins instead. The cost standards LRIC, TSLRIC, TELRIC and FL-LRIC are all forward-looking attempts to price access on what an efficient modern network would cost, not on historic accounts. Notice stand-alone cost reappearing as the ceiling — the same guardrail from the Ramsey slide.

THEME 5 · ACCESS — TERMINATION & ENTRY PATHS

Who pays to connect, and the ladder up.

Termination & interconnection
Termination monopolyEach network monopolises delivery to its own subscribers.
CPNPCalling-party-network-pays: the caller's network pays the terminating network.
RPNPReceiving-party-network-pays: the called party's side bears the cost.
Bill-and-keep (BAK)Networks swap traffic and keep their own revenues; no termination charge.
Waterbed effectCutting regulated termination rates pushes up other (retail) prices.
Zero-charge accessReciprocal termination priced at zero.
Adjacent monopoliesSeparately-owned complementary bottlenecks whose pricing interacts.
Investment & entry paths
Ladder of investmentRegulate so entrants climb from reselling toward building their own facilities.
Stepping-stone hypothesisAccess-based entry as a stage toward facilities-based competition.
Access / regulatory holidayLight or no access obligation to reward risky new investment.
Facilities-based competitionRivals compete with their own networks.
Service-based competitionRivals compete using access to the incumbent's network.
Inter- / intra-platformRivalry across different network technologies vs within one.
Real-option valueThe value of waiting; why irreversible network investment needs a risk premium.
This slide finishes access with two related debates. On the left, the termination problem: every network is a little monopoly over delivery to its own subscribers, so how do you price the handoff between networks? The regimes are named acronyms worth memorising — calling-party-pays, receiving-party-pays, and bill-and-keep, where networks simply keep their own revenues and charge each other nothing. The waterbed effect is the subtle general-equilibrium warning: squeeze regulated termination rates and the operator recoups elsewhere, so retail prices float back up. On the right is the investment-versus-competition dilemma that dominates modern telecoms policy. The ladder of investment and stepping-stone hypothesis argue that cheap mandated access lets entrants start small and climb toward building their own infrastructure. The counter-argument, captured by regulatory holidays and real-option value, is that forcing access on new, risky investment discourages anyone from building it in the first place — because irreversible investment under uncertainty has an option value of waiting that a low regulated return destroys. Facilities-based versus service-based competition is the axis this whole debate turns on.

Institutions & accountability

THEME 6 · INSTITUTIONS & ACCOUNTABILITY

Who regulates — and can we trust them?

TermDefinition
Independent regulatorAn agency insulated from day-to-day political control.
Ex ante regulationSet rules and prices before conduct occurs (utility-style).
Ex post regulationIntervene after the fact via competition law.
Regulatory captureAgency serves the regulated firm's interests over the public's.
Light-handed regulationMinimal intervention, relying on monitoring and the threat to act.
Time-inconsistencyA policy optimal today the regulator is later tempted to abandon.
Credible commitmentBinding the regulator so investors trust prices won't be expropriated.
Expropriation of sunk investmentClawing back returns once capital is committed; deters investment.
Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?"Who guards the guardians?" — the accountability problem for regulators.
Negotiated settlementRegulator brokers an agreed outcome between firm and stakeholders.
These ten terms are about the regulator itself rather than the firms — the governance layer taught in the institutions module. The ex ante / ex post distinction recurs here as the deepest design choice: do you write binding rules up front, the utility model, or do you leave conduct to be challenged afterwards under competition law? The pivotal idea in this cluster is the commitment problem, which ties back to hold-up from the very first slide. Time-inconsistency is the mechanism: a regulator promises high enough prices to induce a firm to sink billions into a network, but once the capital is in the ground and irreversible, the regulator is tempted to cut prices to the bone — expropriating the sunk investment. Rational investors foresee this and won't invest unless the regulator can make a credible commitment not to renege. That is the single most important argument for an independent regulator insulated from short-term politics, and for institutional devices like negotiated settlements. Capture and the Latin tag — who guards the guardians — are the counterweight: independence solves time-inconsistency but reopens the accountability problem.

Key formula

WACC = weighted debt + equity cost

THEME 7 · FINANCE — COST OF CAPITAL

Paying for capital: the allowed return.

WACC. The weighted average of the cost of debt and equity — the return the regulator allows on the RAB. Get it wrong high, consumers overpay; wrong low, the network doesn't get built.
TermDefinition
Cost of capitalThe return investors require to fund the firm's assets.
WACCWeighted average cost of debt and equity; the allowed return on the RAB.
CAPMPrices equity return as risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium.
GearingThe ratio of debt to total capital (leverage).
DCFDiscounted cash flow: values assets as the present value of expected future cash.
DORC / ORCDepreciated / gross optimised replacement cost; a modern-equivalent asset valuation.
RCV / RABRegulatory capital value / asset base; the value on which returns are set.
Modigliani–MillerIn ideal markets, capital structure doesn't change firm value.
Finance is where regulation meets the balance sheet, and for a practitioner it's the most fought-over number at every price review. The whole cluster orbits the WACC — the weighted average cost of capital — because that is the return the regulator allows the firm to earn on its RAB, and it's a knife-edge: set it too high and consumers overpay a monopoly; set it too low and the firm can't finance the investment the network needs, so the lights literally go out over a decade. The building blocks are worth knowing precisely. The cost of equity is usually estimated with CAPM: the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium, where beta measures how much the firm's returns move with the market. Gearing is the debt-to-capital ratio, which matters because debt is cheaper than equity. DCF is the valuation workhorse. DORC and ORC are ways to value the asset base on a modern-equivalent basis rather than historic cost. Modigliani–Miller is the theoretical anchor — in frictionless markets capital structure is irrelevant to value — which is exactly why the real-world deviations from it, taxes and financial distress, are what regulatory finance arguments are actually about.

The final two slides are sector vocabulary — the jargon you need to read a regulator's decision or a trade-press article, taught in the industry modules. Energy first. The pricing question in electricity is fundamentally spatial and temporal because you can't easily store power and the grid congests. Locational marginal pricing, or nodal pricing, prices energy at every node to reflect losses and congestion — the theoretically pure approach used in much of the US; zonal pricing is the coarser European compromise, and Britain is actively debating a shift toward it. Telecoms and water round out the vocabulary. In telecoms the through-line is the shift from the old circuit-switched PSTN to the IP-based next-generation network, which changes the whole cost structure and reopens every access question. CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which definition is stated correctly?

A
The Averch–Johnson effect is a firm's tendency to under-invest in capital under a price cap.

Wrong on both counts — it's over-investment ("gold-plating"), and it arises under rate-of-return regulation, not price caps.

B
Ramsey–Boiteux pricing recovers fixed costs by loading the largest mark-ups onto the services with the least elastic demand.

✓ Correct. The inverse-elasticity rule — least distortion where buyers respond least to price.

C
The ECPR (Baumol–Willig) rule sets the access price equal to the incumbent's stand-alone cost.

No — ECPR = incremental cost plus opportunity cost of access. Stand-alone cost is the price ceiling, a different test.

A three-way self-check across the trickiest clusters. Option A tests two things at once, both classic exam confusions: the Averch–Johnson effect is over-investment, not under-investment — the firm gold-plates because it earns a return on every pound of capital — and crucially it's a pathology of rate-of-return regulation, the very problem RPI–X was designed to cure, so pairing it with a price cap is doubly wrong. Option B is the correct answer and the single most important pricing insight in the course: Ramsey's inverse-elasticity rule loads the biggest mark-ups where demand is least elastic, because those customers cut consumption least, so you recover the fixed costs with the smallest welfare loss. Option C mixes up the two access guardrails: ECPR is incremental cost plus the opportunity cost the incumbent forgoes, whereas stand-alone cost is the ceiling test, the most a service could ever be charged. If a learner picks A, send them back to the price-control slides; if C, back to access pricing. The goal is to feel the difference between these concepts, not just recite the labels.
