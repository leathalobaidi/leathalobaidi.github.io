Welcome to the reference module. Everything else in this course has been about the economics — natural monopoly, price caps, access pricing, cost of capital. This module is about where those ideas actually live in the world: in decided cases, in statutes, in the numbered orders of the FERC and FCC, and in EU directives and ECJ judgments. Think of it as the legal scaffolding that holds the economics up. For a UK practitioner it's genuinely useful, because when you argue an appeal at the CMA or the Competition Appeal Tribunal, you are arguing law as much as economics. We'll move jurisdiction by jurisdiction — USA, EU, UK, then Australia and New Zealand — and finish with five cross-cutting principles that recur everywhere. Treat the tables as a lookup you can come back to; treat the caseboxes as the two or three judgments you should actually be able to cite from memory.

Key terms

ex ante · ex post · duty to supply · judicial review

ORIENTATION · WHY THE LAW MATTERS

Economics proposes; the law disposes.

A regulator's economic reasoning only bites once it is written into a statute, a licence condition, or an order — and it can be overturned by a court. The legal instruments are where regulatory economics becomes enforceable.

Statutes set the mandate

A regulator can only do what its enabling Act lets it do — the Federal Power Act, the Water Industry Act 1991, the Communications Act.

Orders & cases fill the gaps

FERC / FCC orders and decided cases turn broad statutory words like "just and reasonable" into operative rules.

Courts police the boundary

Judicial review and specialist appeals (CAT, CMA) test whether the regulator stayed within its powers and reasoned properly.

The recurring legal question. Where does ex ante sector regulation end and ex post competition law begin? Much of this module is different jurisdictions answering exactly that.
Set the scene before we dive into lists. Why does a course on economic regulation need a module on law at all? Because none of the economics is self-executing. A view about the efficient cost of capital does nothing until it is embedded in a price control, which sits under a licence, which is authorised by a statute — and every link in that chain can be litigated. So three layers to hold in mind. Statutes grant the mandate and confine it: a regulator that acts outside its enabling Act loses on judicial review, full stop. Orders and decided cases do the interpretive work, converting vague statutory standards like "just and reasonable rates" into concrete tests. And courts patrol the edges, checking both the regulator's vires and the adequacy of its reasoning. The single question that threads through the whole module is the ex-ante versus ex-post boundary — when a dedicated regulator writes the rules up front, versus when general competition law polices conduct after the fact. Watch for it in every jurisdiction.

Key term

"Affected with a public interest": the Munn doctrine that lets the state fix prices.

USA · FOUNDATIONS

The constitutional rate cases.

US regulation grew out of a run of Supreme Court cases wrestling with one question: how do you set a price that is fair to customers yet lets the owner earn enough to attract capital?

CaseYearJurisdictionWhy it matters
Munn v Illinois1877US Sup. Ct.Grain elevators "affected with a public interest" may be price-regulated — the origin of the whole US regulatory idea.
Smyth v Ames1898US Sup. Ct.Prices must let the firm earn a return on the fair value of its property — launched decades of "what is fair value?" disputes.
US v Terminal Railroad Assn.1912US Sup. Ct.Early essential-facilities logic: joint control of a must-have bridge/terminal compelled access on fair terms.
Bluefield Water Works v PSC1923US Sup. Ct.The allowed return should match returns on investments of comparable risk — the comparable-earnings idea.
FPC v Hope Natural Gas1944US Sup. Ct.What matters is the end result: any method is fine if the overall rate is just, reasonable and keeps the firm financeable.
These are the cases every regulatory economist half-remembers. Munn v Illinois, 1877, is the constitutional foundation stone: a business "affected with a public interest" — Munn was a grain warehouse — can have its prices fixed by the state without that being an unconstitutional taking. That doctrine is what makes the whole enterprise legal in the US. Then the rate-setting trilogy. Smyth v Ames in 1898 said the owner is entitled to a fair return on the fair value of the property — which sounds reasonable until you realise nobody could agree what "fair value" meant, and it bogged regulation down for forty years. Bluefield in 1923 refined the return side: it should track returns available on comparable-risk investments — that's the comparable-earnings principle that still underpins cost-of-capital work. And Terminal Railroad in 1912 is the sleeper: joint control of the only rail bridge into St Louis triggered a duty to grant access — the seed of essential-facilities doctrine. Hope, on the next slide, is the one that changed everything.

USA · THE PIVOTAL CASE

Hope Natural Gas and the "end result" doctrine.

FPC v Hope Natural Gas Co., 320 US 591 (1944)

The Court abandoned the sterile search for the "right" valuation method. What the Constitution requires is a just and reasonable end result — a rate that is fair to consumers and lets a prudently-run firm maintain its credit and raise capital. Method is the regulator's business; the reviewing court checks the outcome, not the formula.

Why it still matters. Hope is the legal licence for the modern RAB × WACC approach — regulators pick a defensible method (a regulatory asset base, a cost of capital) and defend the result, not a Platonic "true value".
If you learn one US case, learn this one. Before Hope, the Smyth v Ames "fair value" standard had regulators and utilities fighting endless valuation wars — should you value the asset at historic cost, reproduction cost, or something in between? Hope in 1944 cut the knot. The Court said, in effect: stop obsessing over the method. The Constitution doesn't mandate any particular formula. What it requires is that the end result — the actual rate the customer pays — is just and reasonable: fair to consumers, and high enough that a competently-managed utility can maintain its financial integrity and attract the capital it needs to keep investing. That "end result" doctrine is enormously liberating for a modern regulator, and it's why the diagram runs Smyth to Bluefield to Hope and then straight into the RAB-times-WACC machinery this course covered earlier. When Ofgem builds a regulatory asset base and multiplies by a weighted average cost of capital, the legal permission slip for doing it that way — rather than litigating "true value" — traces directly back to Hope.

The boundary

Trinko vs Iowa Utilities Board — where does antitrust yield to the sector regulator?

USA · STATUTES & THE ANTITRUST BORDER

Statutes build the agencies; antitrust sets the edge.

StatuteYearWhy it matters
Sherman Act1890The antitrust backstop — monopolisation is unlawful even without a regulator.
Federal Power Act1935Empowers the FPC/FERC over wholesale electricity & interstate transmission.
PURPA1978Opened generation to independent producers — the first crack in the vertically-integrated utility.
Telecommunications Act1996Interconnection & unbundling duties on incumbents — the charter for FCC open-network rules.
Energy Policy Act2005Modernised FERC's reliability & market-oversight powers.

AT&T v Iowa Utilities Board (1999)

Upheld the FCC's authority to set unbundling rules under the 1996 Act — federal reach into local networks confirmed.

Verizon v Trinko (2004)

Antitrust will not police a regulated firm's access duties where a sector regime already does — a sharp limit on ex-post law.

Frontier. Iowa + Trinko together: ex-ante sector rules do the heavy lifting; antitrust steps back where a regulator is already on the beat.
Two things on this slide: the statutory skeleton and the antitrust boundary. On statutes, note the arc — the Sherman Act of 1890 is the general antitrust backstop that exists whether or not there's a regulator; the Federal Power Act of 1935 and the Communications Act built the sector agencies; PURPA in 1978 is the quiet revolutionary, because by letting independent generators sell power it began prising apart the vertically-integrated utility; and the Telecommunications Act of 1996 is the big one, imposing interconnection and unbundling duties on incumbent local carriers and authorising the FCC's open-network project. Now the boundary, which is the intellectually interesting bit. Iowa Utilities Board in 1999 confirmed the FCC could reach down into local networks with unbundling rules. Trinko in 2004 drew the line the other way: where a sector regulator already imposes and polices access duties, you cannot re-run the same complaint as a Sherman Act monopolisation case. Put them together and you get the American answer to our recurring question — ex-ante regulation occupies the field, and ex-post antitrust defers.

Key term

Open access: the bottleneck owner must carry rivals' energy on non-discriminatory terms.

USA · FERC · THE OPEN-ACCESS STORY

Numbered orders that opened the grid and the pipes.

The FERC's great project was to force owners of transmission and pipelines to move everyone's energy — not just their own — so competition could develop in generation and gas supply.

OrderYearSectorWhy it matters
Order 4361985GasThe "open-access blueprint" — voluntary open access to interstate gas pipelines.
Order 6361992GasUnbundled pipeline transportation from gas sales — the merchant and transport functions split.
Order 8881996ElectricityOpen, non-discriminatory access to the transmission grid; recovery of stranded costs. The landmark.
Order 8891996ElectricityOASIS — a real-time information system so rivals see the same capacity data as the owner.
Order 6372000GasReformed short-term pipeline capacity markets.
Order 20001999ElectricityEstablished Regional Transmission Organisations to run the grid independently of owners.
Order 890 / 7192007/08ElectricityTightened anti-discrimination in transmission service & opened organised wholesale markets to demand response.
This table is the single most useful thing in the module for an energy economist, so let it breathe. The FERC's whole strategy was structural: you cannot have competitive generation if the incumbent that owns the wires can favour its own power, so you force the wires to carry everyone's electrons on equal terms. Read the orders as a story. Order 436 in 1985 was the tentative gas blueprint — open access, but voluntary. Order 636 in 1992 went further and unbundled pipeline transport from gas merchant sales, so the pipe became a common carrier. Then the twin electricity landmarks of 1996: Order 888 mandated open, non-discriminatory transmission access and dealt with stranded costs, and Order 889 built OASIS, the information system that stops the owner hoarding capacity data. Order 2000 in 1999 pushed the logic to its conclusion — hand grid operation to independent Regional Transmission Organisations so the owner isn't refereeing its own competition. Orders 890 and 719 later tightened the screws and let demand response into wholesale markets. If a student remembers one number, it's 888.

UNE · local loop · net neutrality · Open Internet

USA · FCC · ACCESS & NET NEUTRALITY

The same logic, applied to phone and broadband.

The FCC's orders open the incumbent's network — then argue over how far the open-access duty extends into a converged internet world.

OrderYearWhy it matters
Local Competition First R&O1996Implemented the 1996 Act's interconnection & unbundling duties (UNEs).
Cable Modem Declaratory Ruling2002Classified cable broadband as an "information service" — lighter-touch regime.
§251 Unbundling Reviews / UNE pricing2003Which network elements must incumbents share, and at what price?
National Broadband Plan2010Universal-broadband strategy.
Preserving the Open Internet2010The net-neutrality Report & Order — no blocking / undue discrimination.
Protecting the Open Internet (NPRM)2014Re-opened the net-neutrality rules after they were challenged in court.

Access era (1996–2003)

Force the incumbent to lease unbundled network elements to competitors at cost-based prices — classic bottleneck regulation.

Net-neutrality era (2010–)

The open-access idea migrates up the stack: not "share the copper" but "don't discriminate between the data flowing over it".

The FCC story is the same open-access instinct as FERC, but running through telecoms — and it splits into two eras. The access era, kicked off by the 1996 Local Competition First Report and Order, is textbook bottleneck regulation: the incumbent local carrier must lease unbundled network elements — the copper local loop, in particular — to rivals at cost-based prices, and the 2003 reviews fought over exactly which elements and at what price. That's the same logic as FERC Order 888, just applied to phone lines. Then convergence changes the question. Once everything is internet traffic, the interesting bottleneck isn't the physical loop any more — it's whether the network owner can privilege some data over other data. So the open-access principle migrates up the stack into net neutrality: the 2010 Open Internet order says don't block and don't unreasonably discriminate. The 2002 Cable Modem ruling and the 2014 re-proposal show how contested the legal classification is — is broadband a lightly-regulated information service or a common-carrier telecommunications service? That classification fight is really a fight about how far the open-access duty reaches.

Framework / Access / Authorisation / Universal-Service directives · LLU · third package

EU · THE STATUTORY MACHINE

Europe regulates by directive, not by case.

Where the US grew its regime from litigation, the EU built a harmonised framework — directives member states must transpose. Two great strands: electronic communications, and the energy & water internal market.

InstrumentYearDomainWhy it matters
Telecoms Green Paper (COM 87/290)1987TelecomsLaunched EU liberalisation of telecommunications services & equipment.
Services / "full competition" Directives 90/388, 96/191990–96TelecomsProgressively opened telecoms markets to full competition.
2002 framework: 2002/21, /19, /20, /222002TelecomsFramework, Access, Authorisation & Universal-Service Directives — the core e-comms code.
Local-Loop Unbundling Reg. 2887/20002000TelecomsForced incumbents to unbundle the copper local loop for rivals.
Electricity & Gas Directives 96/92, 98/30 → 2003 → third package (2009/72, /73)1996–2009EnergySuccessive internal-market packages — market opening, then ownership unbundling of networks.
Water Framework Directive 2000/602000WaterCommunity framework for water policy & the environmental dimension.
Security-of-gas-supply Reg. 994/2010; Gas network codes (984/2013)2010–13EnergyCommon security-of-supply measures & capacity-allocation rules on gas transmission.
The mental shift crossing the Atlantic is from case law to statute. Europe doesn't wait for a dispute to reach a court; it legislates a harmonised framework and requires every member state to transpose it into national law. Two strands to track. Electronic communications: it starts with the 1987 Green Paper, proceeds through the liberalisation directives of the 1990s, and crystallises in the 2002 package — four directives you should be able to name in outline: the Framework Directive, the Access Directive, the Authorisation Directive and the Universal-Service Directive. Alongside them, the 2000 local-loop-unbundling regulation did in Europe what the FCC's 1996 order did in America — force the copper loop open. The second strand is energy, and it matters enormously for a UK audience: the electricity and gas directives run from the mid-1990s market-opening measures, through the 2003 packages, to the 2009 "third package", whose headline is ownership unbundling — legally separating the monopoly network from competitive generation and supply. The Water Framework Directive folds in the environmental agenda. Europe's whole method is: harmonise, transpose, then let regulators apply.

Art. 102 TFEU · essential facilities · margin squeeze · abuse of dominance

EU · THE CASE LAW

Where the ECJ does shape regulation.

The interesting EU case law is competition law — abuse-of-dominance judgments under what is now Article 102 TFEU that discipline how a dominant network owner treats rivals.

CaseYearWhy it matters
Commercial Solvents (6 & 7/73)1974Early essential-facilities logic — a dominant firm may not cut off an input a rival needs to compete.
RTT v GB-Inno-BM (C-18/88)1991A state telecoms monopoly can't both set the rules and compete — separation of regulator and operator.
Deutsche Telekom v Commission (T-271/03; C-280/08)2008/10Margin squeeze confirmed as a standalone abuse — retail price too close to the regulated wholesale price.
France Télécom v Commission (C-202/07)2009Predatory pricing by a dominant broadband incumbent is an abuse.
Commission v Germany (C-424/07)2009Limits on member-state "regulatory holidays" for new telecoms networks.
The two doctrines to carry away. Essential facilities (roots in Commercial Solvents) — controlling a bottleneck input can create a duty to supply; and margin squeeze (Deutsche Telekom) — granting wholesale access but pricing it so rivals can't profitably retail is itself an abuse.
Europe's directives do the structural work, but the ECJ supplies two doctrines a regulatory economist genuinely needs. First, essential facilities. Commercial Solvents in 1974 is the ancestor: a dominant supplier that stops selling an essential raw material to a downstream rival, in order to take the downstream market for itself, abuses its dominance. Translate that to networks and you have the legal basis for compelling access to a bottleneck. Second, and this is the one that catches people out, margin squeeze. Deutsche Telekom is the key authority: even where the incumbent does grant wholesale access to its network — so there's no outright refusal — if it sets the gap between its regulated wholesale price and its own retail price so narrow that an equally efficient rival cannot make a margin, that spread is itself an abuse. Notice how that closes a loophole: you can't comply with an access duty on paper while defeating it through pricing. RTT v GB-Inno makes the institutional point that a state monopoly can't be both player and referee — which is exactly why the EU insisted on independent regulators.

UK · REGULATION IN THE COURTS

The appeal route a UK dispute actually travels.

Instrument / caseYearWhy it matters
Water Act 19891989Privatised water & created the economic regulator (Ofwat).
Water Industry Act 19911991The consolidating statute — Ofwat's price-control & access duties sit here.
Water Act 20142014Extended retail competition & reformed licensing.
Albion Water v WSRA (CAT, 1046/2/4/04)2006–08The leading UK access-pricing / margin-squeeze dispute — in water, of all sectors — before the Competition Appeal Tribunal.
Albion Water matters because water — long thought competition-proof — became a real test of whether an incumbent's access price left a rival room to compete.
This is the slide closest to a UK practitioner's daily life. The statutes first: the Water Act 1989 privatised water and created Ofwat; the Water Industry Act 1991 is the consolidating Act where the price-control and access duties actually live; and the Water Act 2014 extended competition and reformed licensing. But the memorable item is Albion Water at the Competition Appeal Tribunal. Its significance is almost paradoxical — water is the sector everyone assumes is naturally competition-proof, yet Albion Water turned into the leading British test of access pricing and margin squeeze, litigated for years before the CAT. The diagram is the route to know: a decision or access dispute starts at the sector regulator, Ofwat or Ofgem; a merits appeal goes to the Competition Appeal Tribunal, which can look at the economics properly rather than just judicial-review rationality; and price controls in particular can be referred onward to the CMA for redetermination. That two-tier structure — an expert tribunal plus the CMA — is what makes UK regulatory appeals distinctive, and it's why the economics you argue has to survive real legal scrutiny.

Key term

ECPR: access price = direct cost + the incumbent's forgone margin (Baumol–Willig).

AUSTRALIA & NEW ZEALAND · ACCESS IN COURT

The antipodes stress-tested access pricing.

Case / statuteYearWhy it matters
Commerce Act (NZ)1986NZ's general competition statute — the "light-handed" regulation experiment.
Clear Communications v Telecom NZ1993The famous court test of the ECPR / Baumol–Willig access rule.
Telecommunications Act (NZ)2001Moved NZ from light-handed reliance on general law toward a sector access regime.
Competition & Consumer Act (Aus)2010Australia's consolidated competition & access statute (Part IIIA access regime).
ACCC v Telstra [2010] FCA 7902010Enforcement of access obligations against the telecoms incumbent.

Clear Communications v Telecom NZ (1993)

The Privy Council endorsed an ECPR access charge — direct cost plus the incumbent's forgone retail margin. Efficient in theory (it protects only efficient entry) but fiercely criticised: leaving the incumbent's lost profit in the price can entrench it. This case is why every access-pricing seminar still argues.

The lesson. A "light-handed" regime that relies on general competition law can leave the crucial access price to be fought out case-by-case — often slow and contested. Many jurisdictions moved to explicit ex-ante access rules.
New Zealand ran the boldest natural experiment in regulatory design, and Clear Communications is its emblem. In the early 1990s NZ tried "light-handed" regulation — no dedicated telecoms regulator, just the general Commerce Act and the courts. When Clear tried to interconnect with Telecom, the access-price dispute went all the way to the Privy Council, which endorsed the efficient-component-pricing rule, ECPR — also called the Baumol–Willig rule. ECPR sets the access charge at the direct cost of providing access plus the margin the incumbent forgoes by letting a rival serve the customer. The theoretical appeal is that it only admits genuinely more-efficient entrants. The devastating criticism is that by baking the incumbent's lost profit into the access price, you can preserve its monopoly rents and deter the very competition you wanted. That unresolved tension is why the case is still taught. The broader lesson, reinforced by the later NZ Telecommunications Act and Australia's Part IIIA regime and the ACCC's Telstra enforcement, is that leaving access prices to be litigated case-by-case is slow and uncertain — which pushed most jurisdictions back toward explicit ex-ante access rules.

SYNTHESIS · HOW COURTS SHAPE DISCRETION

Five principles, running through every jurisdiction.

Fair return

Smyth → Bluefield → Hope: set a price that is fair to users yet keeps the monopoly financeable. The legal root of RAB × WACC.

Open access

FERC 636/888, FCC 1996 orders, EU LLU & Access Directive: force the bottleneck owner to carry rivals.

Essential facilities

Terminal Railroad, Commercial Solvents: controlling a must-have input can compel supply on fair terms.

Unbundling

AT&T break-up, Telecoms Act 1996, EU third package: split the monopoly network from the competitive layers.

Antitrust vs ex-ante

Trinko vs Iowa Utilities Board: when does a sector regime displace competition law? Margin-squeeze cases show both can bite.

The single thread

Every principle is one tool for the same job — making a natural monopoly behave as if it faced competition.

Now zoom out. Strip away the jurisdictions and you find the same five principles being reinvented everywhere. Fair return is the rate-case inheritance — Smyth to Bluefield to Hope — and it is the legal permission for everything this course said about the regulatory asset base and the cost of capital. Open access is the FERC-and-FCC instinct, echoed by the EU's local-loop and Access directives: make the bottleneck owner carry everyone. Essential facilities, from Terminal Railroad through Commercial Solvents, is the doctrine that controlling a must-have input can compel you to supply your competitors. Unbundling is the structural move — the AT&T break-up, the 1996 Act, the EU third package — separating the monopoly wires from the competitive services. And the antitrust-versus-ex-ante boundary, crystallised in Trinko against Iowa Utilities Board, decides which body of law does the policing. The unifying thread, and the sentence to end on: every one of these is a different instrument for the same aim — forcing a natural monopoly to behave as though it faced competition it doesn't actually face.
