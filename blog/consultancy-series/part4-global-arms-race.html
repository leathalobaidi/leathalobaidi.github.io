London Goes Global, 2015–2020

Part 4 of 7: international expansion, a control transaction and the limits of the Brexit growth story.

Leath · Revised September 2026

When Analysis Group established its UK company in February 2017, it joined a London market that already contained several American economics firms. Cornerstone Research had added a UK entity in 2014. CRA had opened its London office in 2000; NERA’s London business was older. Brattle’s UK company had been incorporated in 1997. International expansion left a sequence of office, company and acquisition records.

The other direction mattered too. Firms founded in Britain developed offices elsewhere in Europe and beyond. By the end of the decade, a London address could identify one part of a wider business. That complicates the financial comparison: turnover filed by a UK entity need not be revenue earned in Britain.

Building a European practice

Cornerstone Research UK was incorporated on 13 August 2014. Analysis Group Ltd followed on 20 February 2017. These dates establish the UK legal entities. They should not be used automatically as the first day an international firm advised a British client.

CRA illustrates how an office could become a larger practice. Its August 2000 announcement named three practice leaders and about fifteen additional employees. Its UK subsidiary completed the acquisition on 16 June 2005 of Economics of Competition and Litigation Ltd, previously known as Lexecon Ltd. The London company began trading in 1991, and Bill Bishop chaired it by 2005. FTI’s 2003 purchase covered substantially all of the separate US Lexecon’s assets. Acquiring a practice gave CRA another route to growth alongside recruiting individuals.

Compass Lexecon’s European development took a related route. Its March 2011 LECG announcement projected a combined European team of more than forty. That was the expected team after the proposed acquisition, not a count of people newly arriving in London.

These events predate and overlap the period covered here. They explain why the 2015–2020 market was already international. An acquisition, a local office and a newly registered subsidiary leave different records; a history that treats each as a firm’s founding will get the sequence wrong.

Phoenix takes control

In March 2018, Phoenix Equity Partners acquired control of Capital Economics. LDC’s exit release put the business valuation at about £95m. Management, including Roger Bootle, retained a significant holding. Phoenix did not buy the whole company.

LDC had invested as a minority shareholder in 2014. At exit it reported revenue of more than £22.5m in the latest financial year and a return of 2.5 times its money, with a 43% internal rate of return. These were LDC’s reported investment returns; they do not establish what every shareholder earned.

Using £22.5m as a rounded revenue reference gives £95m ÷ £22.5m, or about 4.2 times revenue. Since the release says revenue exceeded that amount, the calculation is an approximate valuation comparison, not a precise transaction multiple. Neither input is the price paid for an individual shareholder’s stake.

The exit announcement described plans to invest in services, technology and acquisitions. Subscriptions meant customers paid repeatedly for research rather than commissioning a fresh investigation each time. The deal showed that a specialist economics-research business could pass from a minority sponsor to a controlling investor while management retained equity.

Later Companies House charges document secured financing. They do not, by themselves, reveal the debt outstanding or prove that ownership changed again. The 2018 transaction is established by the announcements, rather than inferred from a lender’s name.

Brexit as an explanation

The 2016 referendum created questions about the rules under which clients would trade, invest and seek merger approval. It is plausible that uncertainty increased demand for regulatory and political-economic advice. The available accounts cannot isolate that demand from other sources of growth.

Timing complicates that explanation. Flint Global was founded in 2015, before the vote. Public First dates its launch to 2016. Companies House records the incorporation of Hanbury Strategy and Communications that September. These firms sold policy, research or communications advice, but their launch dates alone cannot establish how much of the opportunity came from Brexit.

Flint is a useful example. Its founders included former Ofcom chief Ed Richards and former Foreign Office permanent secretary Sir Simon Fraser. The firm’s offer brought regulatory experience and knowledge of government to commercial clients. Brexit may have enlarged the opportunity; the founding date and subsequent turnover cannot tell us by how much.

For competition specialists, changes to the division of work between British and European authorities could also create additional advice. A 2018 CMA speech discussed the prospect of parallel reviews. Establishing the effect on a particular consultancy would require case or revenue evidence. Growth during the Brexit period is not a measure of growth caused by Brexit.

Scale without a sale

Frontier and Oxera offer a useful contrast with Capital Economics. Frontier retained employee ownership; Oxera operated as an LLP. Oxera’s consolidated accounts for the year to April 2020 reported turnover of £34.2m. The geography note allocated £21.3m to the UK and £12.9m to the rest of the world.

The financial comparison needs the same geographical care as the office history. Turnover describes the reporting entity’s business, which may extend beyond Britain. It does not identify the contribution of Brexit, overseas offices, hiring, fees or changes in case mix.

RBB’s accounts became striking for a different reason. In FY2021 it reported about £60.7m of turnover and twenty average members. The financial comparison belongs in Part 5, where profit, members’ remuneration and the geography of the consolidated business can be examined together. At this point in the history, the important fact is that a competition specialist had reached substantial scale.

What the period leaves us

By 2020, economics advice was being sold through international subsidiaries, domestic partnerships, employee-owned companies and a sponsor-controlled research house. London served as a base for several of these models, without defining the geographical limits of their work.

The next years would bring different transactions again. A McKinsey report describes Vivid Economics and Planetrics as part of the group as of 4 March 2021. A strategic buyer could integrate specialist economics and analytics into a larger advisory business. That route, and the financial rewards within independent firms, are the subject of the next chapter.
