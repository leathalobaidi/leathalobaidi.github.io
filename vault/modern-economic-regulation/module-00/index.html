CHAPTER 1 · WHAT THE BOOK IS

One subject, three essential aims.

An accessible, non-technical entry point that examines how economic regulation is applied in four key public utility industries — electricity, gas, water and telecoms — and asks four fundamental questions: why regulate? what are the alternatives? which institutions? what have been the impacts?

① The rationales

Why economic regulation is applied so widely — and how each rationale relates to actual policies, and to the alternatives to regulation.

② The principles (theory)

The key economic principles for regulating utilities — separating core network (natural-monopoly) activities from the competitive ones around them.

③ The practice

How regulation is actually done — the conceptual approaches to controlling prices and conduct, applied across the four industries with real evidence.

The through-line. Rationale justifies the intervention; theory tells you how it should work; practice shows what regulators actually did — and Decker keeps asking whether the three line up.
Decker states the purpose crisply in his own introduction, and it's worth committing to memory because it explains the whole architecture. The book has three essential aims. First, the rationales — why is economic regulation applied so widely to these industries, how does each rationale connect to specific policies, and crucially, what are the alternatives people have proposed or tried? Second, the principles — the most important results from analytical, theoretical work on how to regulate, built around one big distinction: the "core" natural-monopoly activities, the wires and pipes and cables where there's no competition, versus the activities that are competitive or could be. Third, the practice — a general description of how regulation is done, the main approaches to controlling prices and conduct, and then a close look at four industries with whatever evidence exists on effects. Notice the intellectual honesty in the callout: Decker's recurring move is to hold theory and practice up against each other and note where they diverge. That tension is the spine of the whole course.

1.4 · STRUCTURE OF THE BOOK

Three parts: rationale → principles → practice.

Here is the whole book on one page — burn this image into memory. Part One is the "why": Chapter 2 asks why we regulate at all, weighing the normative case against the more sceptical interest-group explanations, and Chapter 3 asks whether regulation is even the right tool, surveying the alternatives — competition for the market, contestability, state ownership, ex-post competition law, negotiated settlements. Part Two is the "how in principle": Chapter 4 sets out pricing principles for core-network activities, Chapter 5 turns those into the actual instruments — rate of return, price caps and hybrids — and Chapter 6 handles the hard modern problem, access pricing when competition sits alongside a monopoly bottleneck. Part Three is the "how in practice": Chapter 7 covers the institutions, the independent regulators themselves, and then Chapters 8 through 11 walk through electricity, gas, telecoms and water one at a time, each with real evidence. Chapter 12 closes the loop, asking whether the theory of Part Two actually explains the practice of Part Three. Our course modules map one-to-one onto these chapters. The colours here — green for rationale, terracotta for principles, neutral for practice — recur throughout the deck.

1.4 · WHAT EACH PART DOES

From "should we?" to "here's how they did."

Part I · Rationale Ch 2–3

Why regulate (normative and positive), and the alternatives to standard regulation — competition for the market, contestability, state ownership, ex-post competition law, negotiated settlements.

Part II · Principles Ch 4–6

The analytical toolkit: pricing for core networks under full and imperfect information; the forms of price control; and access pricing where competition meets a monopoly bottleneck.

Part III · Practice Ch 7–12

The institutions of regulation, then the four industries in turn, then conclusions on whether restructuring worked and what challenges lie ahead.

Why the ordering matters. You can't judge a price cap (Part II) without knowing the rationale it serves (Part I), and you can't assess a regulator's record (Part III) without both. Each part is the yardstick for the next.
Let me put words to the three columns. Part One is where scepticism lives. Chapter 2 splits the "why" into normative theories — regulation should exist to fix natural monopoly, curb market power, handle externalities — and positive or interest-group theories, which say regulation exists because organised groups demand it. Chapter 3 then refuses to assume regulation is inevitable and lines up the alternatives. Part Two is the engine room, and it's the most technical: Chapter 4 derives pricing principles for the core network, first under the fiction of full information then under the realistic case of imperfect information; Chapter 5 turns principle into instrument — rate of return versus price caps, plus the hybrids; and Chapter 6 tackles access, the defining modern problem, in both one-way and two-way forms. Part Three grounds all of it — Chapter 7 on the regulators as institutions, then the four industry chapters, then Chapter 12's honest reckoning. The callout is the reason for the ordering: each part supplies the standard against which you judge the next. That's why our first real module is Chapter 2, not Chapter 8.

CHAPTER 1 · WHY THESE FOUR

Electricity, gas, telecoms, water.

Each shares the same anatomy: a core network with natural-monopoly attributes — the wires, pipes, cables — wrapped in potentially competitive layers (generation, supply, services). Regulation ring-fences the monopoly bit and lets competition run elsewhere. The four are chosen because restructuring reshaped them most, and the evidence base is richest.

Why not transport too? Decker's "public utility industries" also include transport, but the four industry chapters focus on electricity, gas, telecoms and water — the sectors where restructuring evidence is deepest.
Why does a general book on regulation end with four detailed industry chapters, and why these four? The answer is on the diagram. Every one of these industries has the same underlying anatomy: a core network in the middle — the electricity wires, the gas pipelines, the telecoms cables, the water pipes — which has genuine natural-monopoly characteristics and so gets regulated, surrounded by layers that are, or could be, competitive. In electricity that's generation upstream and retail supply downstream; in telecoms it's content and services around the access network; in gas it's production and shippers around the pipeline. The whole modern regulatory project is about separating — "unbundling" — the monopoly core from the competitive layers, regulating the former tightly and letting rivalry discipline the latter. Decker's formal list of public utility industries includes transport, but the four he devotes chapters to are electricity, gas, telecoms and water, because those are where the 1980s–90s restructuring reforms went deepest and where there's now real evidence to assess. Hold this picture: it's the template for Modules 8 through 11.

CHAPTER 1 · ISSUES OF TERMINOLOGY (1/2)

The words Decker pins down — first pair.

"Public utility industries" — not "utilities"

Decker's collective term for electricity, gas, water & wastewater, transport and telecoms. European texts often say "utilities" or "network industries" for the same set. Telecoms is included because most regulators cover it.

"Core network" — not "natural monopoly"

Older texts called an entire supply chain a "natural monopoly." After unbundling that's wrong. Core network names only the activities with natural-monopoly attributes — the transport layer: pipes, wires, rails, poles.

Why the precision earns its keep. Call a whole industry a "natural monopoly" and you'll over-regulate the competitive bits. The core-network / competitive-layers split is the single most important distinction in the book.
Decker opens Chapter 1 by fixing terminology, and it's not pedantry — different countries use different words for the same thing, and getting them muddled will trip you up later. Two here. First, "public utility industries" is his umbrella term for electricity, gas, water and wastewater, transport and telecoms. Watch out: European writers often just say "utilities" or "network industries" for the same set. And note he deliberately folds telecoms in, even though some don't consider it a utility, because in practice most regulators' remits include it. Second, and this is the big one, "core network" versus "natural monopoly." Many older texts labelled a whole supply chain — all of gas, all of electricity — a natural monopoly. That stopped being true once restructuring unbundled the competitive activities from the monopoly ones. So Decker reserves "core network" for just the activities that retain natural-monopoly attributes: the transportation element, the pipes, wires, rails and poles. As the callout says, this distinction is load-bearing — muddle it and you'll regulate competitive activities you should leave alone.

Watch for

"deregulation" is a misnomer here

CHAPTER 1 · ISSUES OF TERMINOLOGY (2/2)

The words Decker pins down — second pair.

"Restructuring" — not "liberalisation" / "deregulation"

The 1980s–90s policies that changed industry structure: removing entry restrictions, mandating non-discriminatory access to core networks, forcing vertical/horizontal separation. Decker avoids "liberalisation" (ideological overtones) and "deregulation" (inaccurate — regulation was replaced by different regulation, not withdrawn).

"Jurisdiction" — not "country"

An autonomous geographical area — a US state, an Australian province, an EU Member State. Used because different jurisdictions within a country often regulate very differently.

The recurring caution. "Deregulation" almost never meant the state stepped back. It meant a shift from one form of oversight to another — often a bigger regulatory apparatus, not a smaller one.
Two more terms, and both carry a warning. First, "restructuring." This is Decker's neutral label for the wave of policies, mostly in the 1980s and 90s, that changed the structure of these industries — removing statutory restrictions on entry, requiring incumbents to give rivals access to the core network on non-discriminatory terms, and forcing vertical or horizontal separation. He pointedly avoids two common alternatives: "liberalisation," because it carries ideological baggage, and "deregulation," because it's simply inaccurate — regulation wasn't withdrawn and replaced by pure competition; it was replaced by different forms of regulation and oversight. In many places the regulatory state got bigger, not smaller. Second, "jurisdiction" rather than "country." He uses it because the autonomous unit that actually sets regulation is often sub-national — a US state, a Canadian province, an EU Member State — and different jurisdictions within one country frequently take very different approaches. When you read "in some jurisdictions" later in the course, that's precise, not vague. Keep the callout in mind whenever a politician says "deregulation" — it rarely means what it sounds like.

The second reference table splits the sector jargon into energy on the left and telecoms on the right. On the energy side: RPI-X is the price-cap formula, retail price index minus an efficiency factor X, Britain's 1984 innovation — you'll spend real time on it in Module 05. The third table is the analyst's toolkit — the methods and financial concepts that recur across every industry chapter. The financial cluster first: the RAB, the regulatory asset base — also written RAV or RCV depending on the regulator — is the value of the capital the firm is allowed to earn a return on, and setting it is one of the most contested tasks in the job. The return rate itself is the WACC, the weighted average cost of capital, and you estimate the equity part of it with the CAPM. DCF and NPV are the standard tools for valuing cash flows across time — essential once we hit multi-period regulation. Then the cost-and-access cluster: ECPR, the efficient component pricing rule, is a famous formula for pricing one-way access, and LRIC is the forward-looking incremental cost concept that underpins it. Finally the benchmarking methods regulators use to work out how efficient a firm should be: DEA, the non-parametric frontier method, SFA, its parametric cousin, TFP for productivity growth — which feeds directly into the X in RPI-X — and the OLS family of regressions. When any of these surface in a later module, this is your glossary.

HOW THE ARGUMENT FLOWS

One question, followed all the way down.

The habit to build. Every module answers one link in this chain. When you're deep in a price-cap formula, ask which box you're in — and which rationale the intervention is meant to serve.
Here's the argument as a single flow, and it's really one question chased all the way down. Alfred Kahn's advice to regulators — "why am I doing this, is it really necessary?" — sits over the whole thing. The chain goes: why regulate, in Chapter 2; or should we not, given the alternatives, in Chapter 3; if we do, how should we do it in principle, Chapters 4 to 6; who carries it out, the institutions of Chapter 7; and finally, did it actually work, across the four industries in Chapters 8 to 11, with Chapter 12 totting up the score. Notice the colours track back to our map — green for the rationale questions, terracotta for principles, neutral for practice. The teaching habit I want you to build is in the callout: whenever you're deep in the technical weeds of some module — a Ramsey price, an access charge, a WACC calculation — pause and ask which box on this chain you're in, and which rationale from Chapter 2 the intervention is ultimately serving. That's what keeps regulation honest, and it's what this whole course is training you to do.

Hold onto these

the ideas that recur in every chapter

KEY TERMS TO HOLD ONTO

Five ideas that recur everywhere.

Natural monopoly & the core network

One firm cheapest over the relevant range — the reason the pipes and wires get regulated. Module 01.

Ex ante vs ex post control

Rules set up front (a regulator) versus acting after the fact (competition law). Modules 01–03.

Rate of return vs price cap (RPI–X)

The two great families of price control — and the hybrids between them. Modules 04–05.

Access pricing & the bottleneck

Fair terms for rivals to use a monopoly core — one-way and two-way. Module 06.

And the fifth: restructuring itself. The unbundling of competitive activities from the monopoly core is the event the whole book responds to — keep asking, in each industry, how far it went and what it achieved.
Before we finish, five ideas to carry with you — the ones that reappear in almost every chapter, so it pays to have a firm grip early. First, natural monopoly and the core network: the cost condition that makes a single firm cheapest, which is why we regulate the pipes and wires — that's Module 01, our next stop. Second, the ex-ante versus ex-post distinction: do you write the rules before the firm acts, which is what a dedicated regulator does, or do you sue afterwards under competition law? That framing runs through Modules 01 to 03. Third, the two great families of price control — American-style rate of return versus British-style RPI-X price caps — plus the hybrids that blend them, in Modules 04 and 05. Fourth, access pricing: once you've split a monopoly core from competitive layers, on what terms do rivals get to use that core? That's the hard problem of Module 06. And fifth, restructuring itself — the unbundling event the entire book is a response to. In every industry chapter, keep asking two questions: how far did restructuring go, and did it actually deliver? That scepticism is the point.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Why does Decker avoid the word "deregulation"?

A
Because these industries were never regulated in the first place.

False — they have been regulated for over a century in the USA.

B
Because regulation wasn't withdrawn — it was replaced with different forms of regulation and oversight.

✓ Correct. He prefers "restructuring"; "deregulation" wrongly implies the state stepped back, and "liberalisation" carries ideological overtones.

C
Because "deregulation" only applies to financial services, not utilities.

Not Decker's reason — his objection is that the term is simply inaccurate for utilities.

A quick self-check on the terminology, because it's the thing most likely to trip you up in later modules. The question: why does Decker avoid "deregulation"? Answer A is wrong on the facts — far from never being regulated, these industries are among the oldest regulated sectors anywhere, with US commissions dating to the 1880s. Answer C invents a distinction he never makes. The correct answer is B: Decker's objection is that "deregulation" is simply inaccurate for the utilities, because regulation was not withdrawn and replaced by free competition — it was replaced by different forms of regulation and oversight, and often a larger regulatory apparatus. That's exactly why he prefers the neutral word "restructuring," and also avoids "liberalisation," which carries ideological overtones. If you picked A or C, go back to slide 8 — this distinction really does matter, because commentators use "deregulation" loosely all the time and you'll need to translate it whenever you see it.
