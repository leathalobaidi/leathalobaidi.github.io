Collapse, Capital and New Entrants, 2010–2015

Part 3 of 7: an incumbent disappears, a research house attracts private equity and new specialists open.

Leath · Revised November 2026

On 14 March 2011, Compass Lexecon announced plans to acquire LECG’s European competition practice. The announcement named Jorge Padilla, Justin Coombs, Lorenzo Coppi and Neil Dryden. Subject to closing, Compass expected its combined European team to include more than forty competition economists.

LECG reported that the sale closed on 31 March 2011. FTI acquired its European forensics, economics and tax practices in the same transaction. The Compass figure counted competition economists in the projected combined team. The seller’s total of about 100 professionals and staff covered the wider sale.

What survived LECG

LECG had been a substantial international business. A 26 May 2010 release, following its combination with SMART Business Advisory, described more than a thousand professionals in 38 global locations. A later court account of its collapse records practice divestitures in spring 2011 and the subsequent end of its expert consulting business.

The European transfer put LECG’s competition expertise inside the platform FTI had assembled from Lexecon Inc and COMPASS. Their 2008 combination supplied an established American business on which to build.

David Teece had also founded Berkeley Research Group in 2010, before LECG’s collapse. That is a separate event from the named European practice acquisition: the dates, geography and people involved differ.

The episode suggests a feature of expert services: a corporate platform can fail while the expertise remains commercially useful. It does not follow that a practice can move without cost. Client mandates, employment terms and the parent’s finances are separate matters, few of which a launch announcement fully reveals.

A price for subscription research

In October 2014, LDC invested in Capital Economics, taking a minority stake. Roger Bootle’s macroeconomic research firm had a different product from a competition practice: recurring subscriptions to research and forecasts.

LDC’s later exit account says the investment supported international growth and the development of digital services. It reported revenue growth of 30% during its ownership, to more than £22.5m before the 2018 sale. Those are the investor’s figures and account of the strategy.

Institutional capital was already present in broader advisory businesses: Hellman & Friedman invested in AlixPartners in 2006, followed by CVC’s recapitalisation in 2012. Capital Economics matters here because it shows a sponsor backing the subscription economics product itself.

A single deal cannot establish that the whole sector had become an investment theme. It shows that one specialist research house could attract outside equity. The 2018 control transaction would provide a publicly reported valuation.

Different experience, different work

The period also brought specialist firms whose founders had worked outside the established economics boutiques. Their experience helped define what they offered.

Fideres, founded in 2009 by Alberto Thomas and Steffen Hennig, drew on their work in fixed-income markets and structured products at Royal Bank of Scotland. Financial litigation gave that knowledge a different use. In 2014 Thomas gave evidence to the Treasury Committee on benchmark manipulation. The firm places its foundation in the aftermath of the financial crisis; that does not quantify the crisis’s contribution to its revenue.

Fingleton opened in 2013, after John Fingleton’s seven years as chief executive of the Office of Fair Trading. The firm’s own account describes an opportunity to advise senior executives on regulatory strategy. It sold experience of how institutions work alongside analysis of the commercial problem before them.

Flint Global followed in 2015, founded by Ed Richards, Sir Simon Fraser and Nigel Gardner. Richards had led Ofcom; Fraser had been the Foreign Office’s permanent secretary. The firm combined advice on policy, regulation and markets. These were adjacent services to competition economics, and help explain why a directory of firms in this trade resists tidy boundaries.

Their commercial distinction lay in assembling expertise around particular clients’ problems. Experience acquired in a bank or public body could become the basis of an independent advisory business.

Independence remained an option

The arrival of private equity did not settle the ownership question. Frontier continued under employee ownership. RBB retained its LLP structure. Oxera registered its current LLP in April 2014, the year its own history gives for the conversion.

The legal form alone does not determine how a firm grows or whether it can raise capital. Nor does the LLP label identify one uniform financial model. Profit pools, members’ remuneration and the number of people sharing them need separate examination in the accounts.

By 2015, the history contained a failed listed parent, a sponsor-backed subscription firm, independent partnerships and new businesses founded around regulatory or financial experience. The useful question was becoming more precise: which activities fitted which owners?
